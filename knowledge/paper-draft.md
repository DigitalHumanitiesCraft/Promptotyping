## Abstract

Digital research data become accessible only through software, yet generic tools rarely operationalise the distinctions on which project-specific research questions, domain models, and workflows depend. Traditionally, developing software adapted to such requirements has demanded resources that individual researchers and small projects often lack. This paper presents *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for translating structured research data and documented project knowledge into digital research artefacts through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering. Its central organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base of Markdown documents that records the project’s current understanding, including requirements, decisions, procedures, and open questions. LLM-based agents create and revise artefacts from this knowledge base, while findings from implementation, assessment, and verification are written back into it. Each accepted iteration yields an identifiable and versioned *promptotype* that connects the maintained project knowledge, the resulting artefact, the referenced research-data state, and the documented basis of acceptance. Accountable researchers and technical contributors remain responsible for the scholarly and technical judgements through which a promptotype is specified, verified, and accepted. The paper offers a conceptual and methodological consolidation of this practice, developed through experimental practice over two years by a single scholar-developer across documented Digital Humanities projects and teaching contexts.

# 1. Introduction

Scholarly work with digital research data proceeds through software. Research activities such as enrichment, analysis, and dissemination[^1] are carried out through concrete software operations. Software makes data accessible by enabling it to be queried, transformed, related, and presented, while each tool determines which structures and distinctions these operations can address. A CSV file may appear as searchable text, become a sortable table in a spreadsheet, or serve as the basis of a network visualisation in a tool such as Gephi when its records are structured as nodes and edges in the form the tool expects. Relations encoded in a project-specific TEI annotation, by contrast, become a network only after they have been mapped into that input format. This mapping requires decisions about how the project-specific annotation is to be interpreted and represented, together with the technical work of implementing those decisions. Because a generic tool must serve different projects, it can operationalise the mapped data only through the distinctions and operations supported by its own data model. The semantics, context, and uncertainty carried by the project-specific annotation therefore remain usable only insofar as they can be expressed within that generic structure.

Large language models (LLMs)[^2] can reduce the implementation effort required for such transformations. Mapping a project-specific TEI annotation into the input format of a network tool no longer necessarily requires writing the transformation by hand. A model may generate the target format directly, making each execution probabilistic and requiring assessment against the intended mapping. Alternatively, it may generate a transformation script that can be inspected, tested, and rerun deterministically across the corpus. Both approaches continue to require expertise in the source annotation, the intended mapping, and the target representation.

Even a verified transformation, however, establishes only that the chosen mapping has been implemented correctly. It does not establish that the target tool can adequately operationalise the project-specific semantics of the research data, since these remain limited to the distinctions and operations the tool supports. Adapting project data to generic software has often been a pragmatic response to the cost of developing a more closely adapted artefact. LLM-based code generation lowers this barrier because it can also produce the processing, interaction, and interface logic of a project-specific artefact. Rather than adapting the research data primarily to the categories of a generic tool, a specialised artefact can therefore be adapted to the structures, semantics, uncertainties, and scholarly requirements of the project.

This paper introduces *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for translating structured research data and documented project knowledge into project-specific digital research artefacts through LLM-based AI agents. Its contribution is conceptual and methodological. It defines the method, reconstructs its development from documented practice, explains how project knowledge is externalised in maintained documents and made operational through agentic implementation, and establishes how findings from implementation, assessment, and verification are written back into the project knowledge base. Each accepted iteration yields a versioned *promptotype* that connects the maintained project knowledge with the resulting artefact, its referenced research-data state, and the documented basis of acceptance. Promptotyping redistributes implementation work without transferring responsibility for interpretation, verification, and acceptance from the accountable researchers and technical contributors.

The argument proceeds from the problem Promptotyping addresses to the foundations, definition, practical forms, implications, and limits of the method. Section 1.1 examines the relation between structured research data, project-specific digital research artefacts, and Scholar-Centred Design. Section 1.2 establishes the technical and organisational foundations provided by Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, AI harnesses, and Critical Expert judgement. Section 2 reconstructs the development of Promptotyping and defines its recurring forms of work, knowledge architecture, document types, implementation process, verification arrangements, and versioned promptotypes. Section 3 examines the epistemic and methodological implications of translating scholarly knowledge into agent-readable specifications and functional research artefacts. Section 4 presents recurrent artefact forms and the documented projects from which the method was consolidated. Section 5 establishes the empirical scope, conditions of applicability, technical, organisational, infrastructural, ethical, and social limits of the method, sets priorities for evaluation and transfer, and concludes by locating Promptotyping as an amplification of scholarly and technical competence rather than a transfer of scholarly authority to AI agents.

## 1.1 Translating Research Data into Digital Research Artefacts through Scholar-Centred Design

Digital research data broadly include digitally encoded materials produced, collected, or adapted in the course of research. This paper focuses on structured research data: selective representations in which aspects of sources, observations, or research objects are made computationally explicit. Through formal data models—tabular, hierarchical, relational, or graph-based—researchers determine which phenomena are represented and how they can be identified, related, and processed (Flanders and Jannidis 2015). These representations may be implemented in different technical formats and may follow project-specific structures or community standards such as TEI, which can in turn be customised for particular research perspectives (TEI Consortium 2026). Such data do not reproduce their objects in their entirety. They make selected aspects computationally addressable for particular research purposes, while others remain implicit or unrepresented. The way such data are modelled therefore embodies scholarly decisions about what can be distinguished and related, and with what degree of certainty (Drucker 2011; Schöch 2013; Geiger 2024; Pollin 2025a). Software operates on these formally represented distinctions rather than directly on the research objects themselves.

This dependence on selection and modelling is particularly consequential in the humanities. Historical and cultural sources require contextualisation, admit multiple perspectives, and often preserve uncertainty rather than resolving it. Their meaning depends on provenance, material and historical context, and the questions through which they are examined. The same source material may consequently be structured differently for different forms of inquiry. A person, transaction, place, or textual passage does not enter a dataset as a self-evident computational unit. It becomes addressable through scholarly decisions about identification, categorisation, relationships, and the representation of incomplete or ambiguous evidence.

Placing data in a format accepted by generic software does not by itself make the scholarly decisions encoded in their representation operational within that software. A network-analysis tool, for example, can process nodes and edges, but it cannot infer from that structure alone what an encoded relation means for a particular historical question, whether its direction is warranted, or how uncertainty should be represented. Semantic Web technologies can make such meanings more explicit through RDF, ontologies, and provenance information. Yet formalising semantics at the data level does not determine how researchers should work with them in a particular project. Generic tools can process shared structures and explicitly represented semantics, but a research project still requires an operational form adapted to its questions, data, and scholarly practices.

In what follows, *research data* refers specifically to the structured representations that provide the scholarly basis of a project. *Digital research artefacts* are project-specific documentary or technical forms that operationalise those data and the scholarly decisions attached to them for particular research activities. Some are documentary forms that make the project’s data, assumptions, requirements, and decisions explicit. Others implement this knowledge in transformations, processing pipelines, analytical or verification environments, scholarly editions, capture systems, and specialised interfaces. Their defining characteristic is not their technical format but their adaptation to the questions and practices of a research project.

Translating research data into a digital research artefact therefore means more than transferring the same information into another format. It requires decisions about which aspects of the data matter for a particular activity, which operations should be possible, how uncertainty and absence should be represented, and how the results should remain intelligible and verifiable. A digital research artefact is not simply a presentation layer placed over otherwise self-sufficient data. It is a situated operational form that enables researchers to investigate selected aspects of the data in relation to their questions and to develop, test, communicate, and refine scholarly interpretations.

The need for such project-specific forms became apparent in the research undertaken for my dissertation (Pollin 2025a), which brought together work on the *Bookkeeping Ontology*[^3], DEPCHA[^4] (Pollin 2019), and the formulation of Scholar-Centred Design. The ontology made the conceptual structure of historical accounting data explicit, modelling transactions while retaining information about their provenance and uncertainty. DEPCHA used this shared model to normalise heterogeneous datasets and publish them through a common platform. This established an interoperable representational basis, but it did not determine how scholars should investigate the resulting data. Different historical questions continued to require different selections, operations, aggregations, and forms of interaction.

I developed Scholar-Centred Design to translate these differences into requirements for data models, workflows, and interfaces. The approach combined requirements engineering with sustained and iterative engagement with scholars and close examination of their source material (Salinas, Cueva, and Paz 2020; Jetter 2022; Broy and Kuhrmann 2021). It did not merely elicit preferences for a predefined system. Instead, it created a feedback loop in which scholarly understanding informed technical specifications, while modelling and prototyping prompted researchers to articulate and revise their understanding of the sources, data, and domain.

Two contrasting use cases showed how different perspectives on interoperable historical accounting data generated different requirements. Work on the Wheaton Day Book foregrounded the social relationships embedded in economic transactions and called for ways of tracing community networks and forms of exchange. Work on the *Jahrrechnungen der Stadt Basel* foregrounded the hierarchical organisation of municipal accounting and called for aggregation and comparison across its administrative and temporal structures. The shared representation of transactions enabled both forms of inquiry, but it did not determine which distinctions, operations, or interface structures each investigation required.

The dissertation translated these perspectives into requirements and interface concepts adapted to the respective use cases. The relevant distinctions could be represented in the data and specified as desired functions, but the project lacked the development capacity to realise them as usable, question-specific artefacts. This reflects a broader condition in Digital Humanities. Translating scholarly requirements into software has traditionally depended on collaboration between domain specialists and developers, including Research Software Engineers (Edmond 2005; Siemens 2009; Baxter et al. 2012; Cohen et al. 2021; Kemman 2021). Such collaboration remains essential where artefacts require long-term maintenance, integration, institutional deployment, security, or supported use by third parties. Yet the expertise and resources required for conventional software development are not equally available to individual researchers and small projects (Carver et al. 2022).

LLM-based systems can now generate and revise software from structured data, documentary specifications, and project knowledge with substantially less implementation effort than conventional development commonly requires. This does not make data modelling, software engineering, or critical judgement obsolete, nor does it remove the need for professional development and institutional support where systems must remain reliable and maintainable over time. It does, however, make it feasible to turn project-specific scholarly requirements into functional artefacts within the research process rather than deferring implementation to a separate, fully resourced development phase.

The term *Promptotyping* captures this convergence of prompting and prototyping. Project knowledge expressed through maintained documents and instructions becomes the basis for creating, examining, verifying, and revising functional research artefacts. Promptotyping combines Scholar-Centred Design with Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering. Scholar-Centred Design establishes the scholarly requirements and criteria by which an artefact is evaluated. Context Engineering organises and maintains the knowledge required for the work. Agentic Engineering structures how agents use that knowledge to create, inspect, test, and revise documentary and technical artefacts. The next section develops these terms and the technical environment through which they become operational.

## 1.2 Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering

LLMs have become increasingly capable of generating code and carrying out extended software-development tasks. Making effective and responsible use of these capabilities, however, requires more than selecting a more capable model or formulating an effective prompt. Results also depend on the information available to the model, the organisation of the work performed by LLM-based AI agents, and the technical environment through which they interact with a project. These concerns are addressed here through Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI harnesses. None of them removes the need for expert judgement in specifying the intended result and determining whether it is adequate.

*Context Engineering* refers here to the systematic organisation, maintenance, selection, and provision of the information an LLM-based system requires for its work. It extends the concern of *Prompt Engineering* (Schulhoff et al. 2024) from the design of individual inputs and prompting techniques to the wider informational environment in which those inputs are interpreted (Mei et al. 2025). Within Promptotyping, this environment consists of the project’s documented understanding of its research data and domain together with the requirements, decisions, procedures, unresolved questions, and criteria relevant to implementation and verification. Context Engineering does not mean placing all available material into a context window. It creates an inspectable body of project knowledge from which the context required for a particular task can be selected, supplied, and refreshed.

An *AI agent* is understood here as an LLM-based system that pursues a goal through a sequence of tool-supported actions and adapts its subsequent actions to intermediate results (Sapkota, Roumeliotis, and Karkee 2026; Wang et al. 2024). Rather than producing only a response to an isolated input, an agent may inspect project resources, act upon them, evaluate feedback, and continue its work across several steps. Recent literature has begun to use *Agentic Engineering* for the systematic design and organisation of such extended work (Cao 2026; Alenezi 2026). The term remains emergent and is often discussed more narrowly as *agentic programming*, *agentic coding*, or *Agentic Software Engineering*. This paper uses *Agentic Engineering* in a broader sense because the agents involved in Promptotyping work not only on code but also on the documentary and conceptual artefacts through which a research project is described, implemented, and assessed. Agentic Engineering determines how their work is decomposed and continued, how tools and feedback enter the process, and where expert intervention and verification are required.

An *AI harness* is the operational environment through which an LLM-based AI agent receives context, accesses project resources, uses tools, executes actions, and obtains feedback on their results. It mediates the relation between the model and the environment in which the work is performed. Recent work consequently locates agentic capability not in the model alone but in the combined model–harness–environment system (Zhong and Zhu 2026). A harness may provide controlled access to files, repositories, execution environments, browsers, tests, and running artefacts while maintaining task state, permissions, and evidence of the actions performed. It does not itself determine which project knowledge is relevant or how the work should be organised. Instead, it supplies the technical conditions through which Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering become operational.

Promptotyping is not tied to a particular harness or degree of agent autonomy. Its earliest cases were conducted through sequences of prompts under continuous human direction. Contemporary repository-based harnesses enable agents to inspect project documents, modify files, execute commands, evaluate tool responses, and continue work across longer spans between interventions. The methodologically relevant change is therefore not only an increase in model capability. It is an increase in the amount and variety of work that can be performed from a maintained project context before another person must supply the next instruction.

Promptotyping integrates these technical arrangements with Scholar-Centred Design and the continued involvement of a *Critical Expert*. Scholar-Centred Design provides the methodological process through which scholarly understanding is translated into requirements and criteria for evaluating a digital research artefact. Context Engineering maintains this understanding in a form that can guide implementation. Agentic Engineering structures how AI agents act upon it through the facilities of an AI harness. The Critical Expert remains accountable for deciding whether the documented understanding is sufficient, whether the agent’s work corresponds to the research data and purpose, and whether the resulting artefact can be accepted.

The Critical Expert is not necessarily a single person. Scholarly judgement about sources, interpretation, and representational adequacy may be distributed among domain researchers, while technical and agentic-engineering judgement may be held by developers, Research Software Engineers, or hybrid scholar-developers. What defines the role is not individual authorship of every project component but responsibility for the judgements through which project knowledge and artefacts are specified, challenged, verified, and accepted.

Expertise is therefore not replaced by increasingly capable models, larger context windows, or longer agentic workflows. Its role shifts towards specification, contextual judgement, orchestration, diagnosis, and verification. Agentic systems may propose categories, mappings, implementations, tests, and evaluations, but producing a plausible proposal and taking responsibility for its scholarly and technical adequacy remain different acts.

As the span of work between expert interventions increases, the knowledge required to guide that work can no longer remain confined to an individual prompt, a transient conversation, or continuous human direction. It must remain available across actions, sessions, models, and contributors while being open to inspection, contestation, and correction. Promptotyping therefore organises the relation between scholarly expertise, Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and the AI harness around an evolving, inspectable, and versioned project knowledge base. The following chapter reconstructs how this arrangement developed and defines the method through which it is made operational.

# 2. Promptotyping

Promptotyping developed through a series of LLM-supported experiments in research and teaching beginning in 2023.[^5] Early cases used GPT-4 with Advanced Data Analysis to generate Python analyses and visualisations from structured research data. A parallel line of work applied prompt engineering to the creation of structured research data itself, converting unstructured correspondence texts from the Hugo Schuchardt estate into TEI XML.[^6] The conversion relied on a documented mapping table that externalised the transformation rules in an inspectable form, an early instance of the externalisation of project knowledge that the method later consolidates as Distillation. A methodologically important step occurred in work with psychological research data presented at the Doctoral Congress of the Institute of Psychology at the University of Graz in November 2023.[^7] Rather than instructing the model to produce visualisations directly, I asked it to examine the dataset and codebook, relate them to the research questions, formulate user stories, and use those user stories as context for the subsequent visualisations. The user stories provided an intermediate specification that connected the scholarly purpose of the analysis with the structures available in the data and the analytical or visual operations to be implemented.

A further line of development concerned the selection and condensation of project knowledge. Extensive source descriptions, formal models, and instructions could not simply be accumulated in an individual prompt. They had to be reorganised into compact representations that retained the distinctions relevant to a task while remaining inspectable by researchers. An important example was Semantic Markdown, which I used to remodel the Bookkeeping Ontology for work with an LLM. The representation retained classes, definitions, properties, domains, ranges, cardinalities, external alignments, and domain rules while omitting serialisation syntax and repeated formal structures that were not required for the conceptual task. Semantic Markdown was therefore not merely a shortened account of the ontology. It was a task-specific representation designed for a different addressee and use.

The outputs of these early experiments could be discussed with the model and revised iteratively. Parts of the procedure were subsequently transferred into a project-specific Custom GPT that combined instructions, project knowledge, and data-analysis capabilities. Further work externalised additional knowledge in project documents recording data structures, requirements, design decisions, implementation rules, and verification criteria. I first used the term *Promptotyping* in my dissertation and developed the concept further in two subsequent public contributions.[^8] As LLM-based coding systems developed into agents operating through an AI harness within a project-specific Git repository, they gained direct access to project documents, research data, code, tools, execution environments, and running artefacts. They could therefore continue implementation work across several actions without requiring each next step to be supplied through a new prompt. The documents consequently ceased to function only as contextual material for individual interactions. They became an evolving and versioned project knowledge base from which implementation could proceed and into which findings from implementation and verification could be written back. Section 2.1 defines the method in its consolidated form.

## 2.1 Definition

Promptotyping is an iterative, knowledge-driven method for translating structured research data and documented project knowledge into digital research artefacts through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering. Its central organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base maintained through a set of interrelated Markdown documents.[^9] Guided by this knowledge base, LLM-based AI agents create and revise documentary and technical artefacts, while findings from implementation, assessment, and verification are written back into the knowledge base. Responsibility for specifying, verifying, and accepting the resulting artefacts remains with the accountable researchers and technical contributors.

The research data and the project knowledge base fulfil different functions. The research data remain available as the project’s source basis and are processed through explicit transformations, queries, and computational operations. The knowledge base records how those data are understood and used within the project. It contains descriptions of their structure, semantics, provenance, exceptions, and uncertainty together with scholarly purposes and requirements, design decisions, procedures, unresolved questions, and verification criteria. Its individual documents are referred to as *Promptotyping Documents* and are differentiated by function in Section 2.3.

Within Promptotyping, Context Engineering organises, maintains, and supplies the project knowledge required for a particular task. Agentic Engineering structures how agents use that knowledge to work on project documents, research data, code, tools, and running artefacts. The context supplied to an agent therefore remains selective and task-specific even when it is derived from a more extensive project knowledge base.

The knowledge base does not authorise itself. It governs implementation only insofar as accountable researchers and technical contributors inspect, contest, revise, and approve its contents. Where scholarly and technical responsibilities are distributed across several people, the knowledge base provides a shared and revisable object through which their judgements can be documented, compared, and coordinated.

*Promptotyping* applies to documentary and technical artefacts through which modelled research data can be investigated, transformed, enriched, verified, or revised. These range from models and processing pipelines to capture tools, scholarly editions, analytical interfaces, and software applications; no particular output format or technical architecture defines the method. Its application requires research material to be available in a structured and machine-actionable form before it can serve as the basis of implementation. A CSV or JSON file may provide sufficient structure for a bounded project, while markup standards, metadata models, graph-based representations, and ontologies may express richer semantics, provenance, constraints, and uncertainty.[^10] Formal richness alone, however, does not determine a usable research artefact. Data models and standards describe what has been represented, but they do not decide which distinctions should be foregrounded for a particular research question, which operations should be supported, or how their results should be presented and examined. These decisions must be developed and documented within the project.

A *Promptotyping iteration* may contain several internal cycles of Distillation, Implementation, assessment, and verification. It concludes when the relevant project knowledge, the artefact derived from it, and the documented basis of acceptance form a coherent, identifiable, and versioned state for the intended purpose. This accepted iteration state is a *promptotype*. A promptotype therefore does not denote only the visible interface or generated code. It connects the maintained project knowledge, the digital research artefact, the documented basis of acceptance, and a reference to the research-data state on which the artefact operates. It may remain internal, be published, support a handover, or become the starting point for a subsequent iteration.

Earlier notebooks, Custom GPT workflows, FORGE, and comparable experiments are treated as precursors unless the surviving record permits an identifiable accepted iteration state to be reconstructed. An exploratory interface, prompt sequence, or generated codebase is not a promptotype merely because it was produced with an LLM. Later repository-based projects instantiate the consolidated method more fully, while early cases document the development of individual operations such as externalisation, condensation, intermediate specification, and iterative implementation.

The method also requires access to the expertise needed to specify and verify the artefact. An LLM may propose mappings, categories, analytical operations, visualisations, or interface structures, but the Critical Expert determines whether they correspond to the sources, the data model, and the research purpose. Implementation work may be delegated or redistributed, while responsibility for modelling, contextual judgement, verification, and acceptance remains with the accountable contributors.

Promptotyping addresses a bounded part of the capacity gap between scholarly specification and technical implementation. It does not replace professional software development or Research Software Engineering. When an artefact requires dependable operation, long-term maintenance, institutional deployment, security provisions, integration into shared infrastructure, multi-user functionality, or supported use by third parties, it enters the domain of Research Software Engineering. Promptotyping Documents may support this transition as an inspectable handover package, but they do not remove the technical and organisational obligations associated with it.

The method is organised around four recurring forms of work named Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, and Implementation. Each has a distinct object and completion condition, but they do not form a rigid sequence. Preparation establishes the research data, sources, standards, and research context from which the artefact will be derived. Exploration examines what the data can support, what they cannot support, and which assumptions or additional modelling are required. Distillation expresses the resulting understanding in Promptotyping Documents. Implementation makes these documents actionable through an LLM-based AI agent operating within an AI harness.

Throughout the following sections, the Stefan Zweig Digital HTR pipeline serves as a recurring illustration of the method at production scale. The material includes manuscripts, typescripts, correspondence, life documents, works, and essay files described through TEI metadata. The project aims to produce machine-readable diplomatic transcriptions that make the estate readable and searchable without normalising its textual features.[^11]

## 2.2 Preparation and Exploration

**Preparation** gathers and structures the materials from which the artefact will be derived before implementation decisions are fixed. These materials include research data in their original formats, documentation of standards and data models, source descriptions, research questions, domain knowledge, and any existing editorial, annotation, or mapping guidelines. Preparation also establishes the project repository and makes the relevant source material accessible for direct inspection and deterministic processing.

Requirements Engineering belongs at this point because an artefact cannot be specified independently of the scholarly activities it is meant to support. Requirements may be developed from the researcher's own domain knowledge or through collaborative Scholar-Centred Design sessions. Epics and user stories translate research practices into statements that can guide implementation while retaining their scholarly purpose. A user story such as "As a social historian, I want to trace people and their relations through commodity transactions so that I can investigate the social structure of the community" is simultaneously a statement about historical inquiry and a functional requirement. Where requirements are expressed as user stories, established criteria such as the QUS framework distinguish qualities that can be tested formally from those that require an understanding of their content (Lucassen et al. 2016).

The first Promptotyping cases drew directly on the requirements work described in Section 1.1: the Wheaton Day Book and the Basel municipal accounts had been modelled through a shared ontological framework, but their research perspectives required distinct artefacts adapted to their questions and analytical practices. The experimental Wheaton network visualisation was an early case in which code generation, modelled data, and requirements developed through Scholar-Centred Design converged in a research-specific interface. As Figure 2 shows, persons, institutions, places, and commodities were represented through differentiated nodes and relations, while the controls exposed categories derived from the data model and the social-historical user stories. The interface was therefore not a generic rendering of an existing graph. It operationalised a particular prosopographic interpretation of the accounting data.

In the SZD pipeline, Preparation brought together the facsimiles, TEI metadata, source typology, transcription purpose, and available standards. The sources vary substantially, from handwritten Kurrent through typescripts, forms, tables, and proofs to newspaper material and heterogeneous compilations, and these distinctions determine which transcription instructions and processing routes are appropriate. The object metadata was therefore not treated as ancillary catalogue information. It became machine-actionable context that selected prompt variants and governed how individual materials entered the pipeline.

Preparation does not require all research questions to be settled permanently. Scholarly inquiry is often non-linear, and interaction with data and provisional artefacts may refine the initial questions. It does require the current purpose, source basis, and assumptions to be explicit enough that later changes can be identified as revisions rather than absorbed invisibly into implementation.

Vagueness at this point propagates. An undefined category may become an arbitrary filter; an unexplained absence may be rendered as zero; an uncertain date may become a precise point; and a generic instruction to "show relationships" may produce directionality or causal implications not warranted by the data. Preparation establishes the material and conceptual boundary within which Exploration can proceed. It is sufficient when the repository exists, the relevant source data and documentation are accessible, and the initial research purpose and requirements are explicit enough to support systematic exploration.

**Exploration** examines the relation between the research question and the available data. Its central question is not merely whether an interface or analysis can be built, but what the data can support, what they cannot support, and which additional modelling or contextualisation would be required. Knowing what the data cannot support is as important as identifying what can be built from them.

Exploration proceeds through three complementary modes. The researcher inspects the data directly. Deterministic scripts may traverse the full corpus and render compact aggregations, inventories, anomalies, value distributions, or schema-versus-corpus comparisons. The LLM may propose mappings between fields and analytical categories, suggest alternative representations, identify apparent gaps, and generate provisional interface concepts. These proposals are hypotheses about the relation between data and inquiry, not findings that can be accepted without domain judgement. Where generated proposals concern representation and interface design, the resulting decisions are recorded in the design documents developed during Distillation; their epistemic implications are examined in Section 3.1.

In the SZD pipeline, Exploration tested vision-language-model transcription against different hands and object types. The central question was not only which model produced the most plausible text, but where authoritative checking should enter the workflow — a finding whose consequences are taken up in Sections 2.3 and 2.4.

Exploration varies in depth. If the researcher already knows the data and has previously developed the requirements, the work may consist mainly of making that understanding explicit for the agent. In unfamiliar or heterogeneous projects, Exploration may require substantial data profiling, experiments, and comparison of alternative mappings. It ends with a documented account of feasibility, limits, relevant alternatives, and unresolved questions.

## 2.3 Distillation into Promptotyping Documents

**Distillation** turns the understanding produced through Preparation and Exploration into the project knowledge base from which implementation proceeds. It is the principal documentary operation of Context Engineering within Promptotyping. Promptotyping determines which descriptions of sources, semantics, requirements, decisions, constraints, and unresolved questions an agent needs for a particular task, while leaving the fuller research data and source materials available for direct processing and inspection.

The origin of this practice lies in the Semantic Markdown remodelling of the Bookkeeping Ontology described in the opening of this chapter. Its methodological significance for Distillation lies not in the compression it achieved but in the kind of work it required. The full OWL and RDF representations of the ontology contained the formal structures required for interoperability, validation, and machine reasoning, but also namespaces, repeated axioms, and serialisation syntax that consumed context without contributing equally to the conceptual task performed with the model. Context-window constraints made selection necessary, but they did not determine what should be selected. That remained a scholarly and modelling decision. Distillation is therefore better understood as pragmatic modelling than as simple compression: it creates a task-specific representation that preserves the distinctions required for a particular use while leaving the fuller source and formal model intact.

This practice established a central arrangement of Promptotyping. The LLM reads about the data and writes code that reads the data. The research data remain available as the project's source basis. What enters the model's working context is a distilled account of their structure, semantics, exceptions, and uncertainty, together with the research questions, requirements, design decisions, and feedback needed for the current task. The generated code then processes the data through explicit transformations and operations.

Research on long-context degradation shows that nominal context capacity does not imply uniform or reliable use of all the information it contains (Hong, Troynikov, and Huber 2025). Promptotyping responds by selecting, structuring, separating, and refreshing context rather than treating accumulation as a substitute for context design. The practical objective is to retain the maximum task-relevant information while avoiding material that consumes context without contributing to the work. A description adequate for generating a statistical overview may not be adequate for modelling uncertainty, constructing an edition interface, or verifying a transformation pipeline.

The completion criterion is practical: a new agent instance, supplied with the relevant knowledge base and access to the data, should be able to understand the project's current logic and continue the work without an undocumented oral explanation. This criterion cannot guarantee that nothing is missing. It makes omissions visible when the agent cannot act, produces implausible output, or requires information that the project has not yet made explicit.

A **Promptotyping Document** is a Markdown document within the project knowledge base. It represents selected knowledge about the research data, domain context, requirements, design, process, agent behaviour, or verification in a form that human contributors can inspect and maintain and LLM-based agents can use as context. Promptotyping Documents are revised as the project's understanding develops. Together, they provide the documented basis from which digital research artefacts are created and against which generated outputs can be assessed.

Markdown is not epistemically privileged, nor does it provide a formal guarantee of precision. Its role is practical. It is directly readable without specialised software, versionable in Git, processable by current coding agents without a conversion layer, and sufficiently structured to express headings, lists, tables, examples, metadata, and cross-references. It therefore reduces the format boundary between the scholar's description and the agent's working context.

Promptotyping Documents are dually readable, but dual readability does not require every document to address researchers and agents in the same way. The scholar reads to determine whether data, requirements, and decisions have been represented appropriately. The agent reads to determine how to act. A description too vague for the agent to implement against is often also too vague to count as adequate scholarly documentation. Conversely, a technically executable instruction may remain scholarly inadequate if its assumptions cannot be inspected or justified.

Across the documented projects, a recurring core set has stabilised:

- **`data.md`** describes the structure, semantics, formats, provenance, idiosyncrasies, gaps, and uncertainties of the research data. It may include compact examples, element or field inventories, mappings to standards, controlled vocabularies, and warnings about exceptional cases.  
- **`requirements.md`**, sometimes named `specification.md` or `user-stories.md`, expresses functional and non-functional requirements through user stories and acceptance criteria. It connects scholarly practice to implementable and verifiable behaviour.  
- **`design.md`** records interface, interaction, and visualisation decisions together with their reasons. It is where the project can reject model defaults and state how uncertainty, missingness, hierarchy, comparison, and provenance should be represented.  
- **`journal.md`** records the course of the work: sessions, attempted approaches, failures, model and tool use, decisions, unresolved questions, and handovers. It makes the process resumable and later criticisable.  
- An **action document**, such as `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, or an equivalent repository configuration, specifies how the agent should work. It may define the technology baseline, testing strategy, file boundaries, coding conventions, permissions, verification checkpoints, and required documentation behaviour.

Not every project requires each file at equal depth, and filenames are not constitutive of the method. A small repository may combine several functions in one document; a complex pipeline may require a document for every processing stage. What matters is that the relevant knowledge functions are present and that their status and provenance are clear.

The design document is where the project relocates representation decisions from model defaults into explicit and inspectable specifications. It may require that dating uncertainty remain visible as an interval, that missing values not be silently excluded, that a graph not imply directionality absent from the sources, or that contested categories remain distinguishable from factual classifications. The purpose is not to guarantee that the artefact will be epistemically adequate. It is to make the project's representational decisions available for scrutiny and correction.

In the SZD pipeline, Distillation translated the exploration findings into declarative and action documents. An annotation protocol defines the transcription conventions and records their rationale: uncertain readings, illegible passages, deletions, insertions, and other interventions must remain visible through defined markup rather than being silently normalised. A verification concept specifies the ground-truth strategy, external quality signals produced independently of the transcription step, cross-model comparison, and the authority attached to different checks. TEI metadata determines the automatic assignment of object-specific context, and the design requires every transcription to be displayed beside its facsimile so that claims about the text remain inspectable against the source.

As the knowledge base expanded, different kinds of knowledge had to be kept apart. Figure 3 distinguishes documents that describe the project, documents that preserve the course and rationale of the work, and documents that direct agent behaviour. The distinction adapts the established separation between declarative and procedural knowledge and adds a process layer for preserving how and why the work developed. It protects relatively stable scholarly descriptions from volatile implementation instructions and provides a practical way to locate the source of an error.

![Three functional classes of Promptotyping Documents: declarative, process, and action documents.](assets/figures/figure-2-document-types.png)

**Figure 3.** Three types of Promptotyping Document. Declarative Documents state what is currently understood about the sources, data, requirements, design, and verification. Process Documents record how the work proceeded and why decisions were made. Action Documents govern how agents should act within the project.

**Declarative Documents** state what is the case according to the project's current understanding. They include data descriptions, requirements, design decisions, editorial guidelines, mapping rules, verification concepts, and descriptions of project pipelines. They contain the scholarly and system knowledge against which output is assessed. Their content should survive changes in code, model version, or tool because it expresses the maintained project description rather than one particular execution.

**Process Documents** state how the work proceeded and why. The journal is the primary example, supplemented in larger projects by decision records and curated learnings. Together with Git history, these documents form a context memory across sessions. Commits show what changed; process documents explain why, which alternatives were considered, and what remains unresolved.

**Action Documents** state how to act. They direct the agent's behaviour, define the technical baseline, establish tests and checkpoints, and specify role or permission boundaries in multi-agent projects. They are intentionally separated from stable domain descriptions because action instructions are more volatile. A temporary implementation directive should not silently overwrite a maintained statement about what the data means.

The typology is analytical rather than rigid, but it has a diagnostic function. When an output is factually or conceptually wrong, the declarative layer should be inspected. When it violates an intended format, workflow, technology, or style, the action layer may be incomplete. When the rationale for a decision is unclear or inconsistent, the process layer should be consulted or updated. The distinction directs corrections towards the kind of knowledge that failed rather than accumulating local patches in the code.

A second distinction concerns provenance rather than knowledge type: documents may be **curated** or **generated**. Curated documents contain judgements maintained by the Critical Expert, even when an LLM assists with drafting or restructuring them. Generated documents are deterministically rendered from source data by a named script or command. The practical test is whether the next execution of that command will overwrite the file.

Generated documents are particularly useful for large datasets. A script can traverse a TEI corpus and produce compact reports on element usage, attributes, anomalies, distributions, controlled values, or mismatches between schema and corpus. The report can be committed and made available to the agent without repeatedly placing the full corpus in context. It remains traceable to the source and can be refreshed when the corpus changes. Generated documents make Distillation a build-time stage as well as a scholarly writing practice. Such documents should be clearly marked and should not be edited manually.

Because Promptotyping Documents may themselves be drafted, revised, or generated with model assistance, their provenance belongs to the method. A document should disclose whether its claims are curated by a person or rendered by a named process, and under which model and tool conditions it was produced. Model assistance does not make a document generated in this sense if a person remains responsible for the judgement it formulates.

The `knowledge/` folder collects the project's curated and generated knowledge layer. It is the part of the work that outlasts the current chat or agent session and that can be read, checked, cited, and handed over. Its completeness depends on a discipline of writing back. Implementation inevitably produces local decisions, corrections, and discoveries. They become durable project knowledge only when incorporated into the relevant document.

The public Promptotyping templates consolidate document functions that recurred across multiple projects. They remain revisable as further use shows which distinctions projects can sustain in practice. Documents may declare the template they instantiate through a persistent reference, allowing readers and agents to retrieve the current specification of that document function.

The templates also include **Verification Documents**. These record a claim, the materials against which it was checked, the procedure used, and the resulting verdict. Verification can therefore apply not only to generated artefacts but also to factual claims made about projects and the method. Selected factual claims in this paper are accompanied by corresponding records in the companion repository.

Promptotyping Documents differ from project documents written primarily for compliance or retrospective reference. Whereas machine-actionable data management plans are intended to expose planning information to infrastructure (Miksa et al. 2019), Promptotyping Documents are operative in implementation sessions: agents plan, generate, and test from them, and documents that do not support that work are revised.

## 2.4 Agentic Implementation and Verification

### Agentic implementation

**Implementation** makes the evolving knowledge base actionable through an LLM-based agent operating within a project environment. The agent may translate the semi-formal project description into data transformations, models, schemas, tests, code, interfaces, and revised knowledge documents. The AI harness mediates this work by providing access to project files, tools, execution environments, task state, permissions, feedback, and verification facilities.

Agentic Engineering, as introduced in Section 1.2, organises this work: the agent becomes an executing instance, while human work shifts towards specification, orchestration, contextual judgement, and verification. The methodologically relevant variable is the span of work performed between two human interventions. The longer that span becomes, the more of the project’s intent, constraints, and evaluation criteria must be represented in the maintained knowledge base before and during implementation. Under continuous prompt-level direction, the researcher carried the coherence of the work, supplied missing context, and decided each next step. Repository-based agents can instead inspect files, modify several components, execute commands, read tool responses, and pursue an implementation goal across multiple steps. The knowledge base therefore no longer serves merely as material attached to a prompt. It provides the maintained reference from which the agent plans and implements and against which its work can be inspected.

This arrangement places Promptotyping within the broader movement of **Spec-Driven Development** (Macedo 2026). Agentic software-development frameworks increasingly use persistent specifications as the governing reference from which agents plan and implement, separating stated intent from generated code. Promptotyping shares this commitment, but the content and authority of its specifications differ. Promptotyping Documents also describe sources that precede the system and carry their own semantics, uncertainty, provenance, and scholarly history. The researcher is therefore not only a stakeholder commissioning functionality. They hold epistemic authority over statements about the research material.

This is the principal difference from **Vibe Coding**, understood as generating code from natural-language instructions while accepting its implementation without thorough review (Sarkar and Drosos 2025). Promptotyping shares the possibility of rapidly generating functional code and retains an exploratory mode in which alternatives can be produced before requirements are settled. It differs through the preparation of data and context, persistent specifications, versioned process memory, and defined verification checkpoints. A provisional interface may be generated quickly to expose possibilities, but it does not become an accepted promptotype merely because it runs.

### Validation, review, and verification

The knowledge base constrains implementation without determining it completely. Different agent runs may realise the same requirements differently, and a precise specification does not guarantee an adequate result. Implementation therefore combines generation with several forms of feedback.

**Deterministic feedback** comes from schemas, constraints, test suites, linters, and builds. These checks can establish whether an output is well formed, whether expected files exist, whether functions return specified values, or whether the project still builds after a change. Their decision rules are sufficiently formal to run without human judgement.

**Visual feedback** comes from the running artefact. Screenshots allow the agent and researcher to inspect layout, labels, interactions, missing elements, and inconsistencies between specification and rendering. Visual inspection is necessary because many failures are not represented in formal code correctness: content may be clipped, hierarchy may be unreadable, a filter may technically work but communicate the wrong relation, or a conventional representation may contradict the design principles.

Agents can also contribute to checking. They may compare generated outputs with sources or stated rules, operate a running interface against user stories and acceptance criteria, and report the results. Such **agentic review** extends the coverage of checking between deterministic validation and expert verification, but it never authorises: an LLM-mediated comparison remains a generative operation, not scholarly acceptance. The limits of such review became concrete in the ZBZ OCR/TEI pipeline, where an agent-based screening stage had recorded approval statuses that no human had authorised, effectively collapsing production and verification into one generative operation. Decision E66 abolished agent screening and introduced per-stream workflow status instead.[^zbz-e66]

**Verification by the Critical Expert** applies where correctness depends on scholarly judgement. The Critical Expert determines whether the data has been represented appropriately, whether the artefact addresses the research question, whether an interpretation is warranted, and which context a result requires before it can be read. This authority is not delegated.

In the SZD pipeline, this separation of checking forms is made explicit in the artefact itself. Verification is represented through three statuses. A human-checked transcription, for which each page has been read against its facsimile, has the highest authority. An agent-checked transcription indicates that a vision-language model compared image and transcript. Unchecked material remains explicitly marked as such. The statuses do not collapse distinct forms of checking into a generic confidence score. Human corrections preserve the machine-generated transcription in an edit history, making intervention traceable and preventing the accepted text from appearing as though it had been produced without revision.

More generally, this paper distinguishes **deterministic validation**, in which a formal rule decides whether a condition has been met, from **Critical Expert verification**, in which acceptance depends on domain judgement (IEEE 2017). Table 2 summarises the forms of checking and the autonomy an agent may exercise at each.

**Table 2. Forms and objects of checking in Promptotyping.**

| Object of checking | Form of checking | What decides | Agent autonomy |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Deterministically decidable data fidelity | Validation | Schemas, tests, constraints, builds | The agent may run the checks and act on unambiguous failures |
| Source fidelity requiring judgement | Verification | Comparison with sources by the Critical Expert | Agentic review may assist but remains subordinate |
| Requirement satisfaction | Acceptance testing | Acceptance criteria in the requirements document | An agent may operate the artefact and report results |
| Design conformance | Verification | Critical Expert against the design document | Requires human assessment |
| Interpretation and contextualisation | Verification | Scholarly judgement against sources and research context | Cannot be delegated for final acceptance |

Acceptance criteria allow part of requirement checking to be delegated. An agent can operate a running interface, test whether specified controls exist, and report whether an expected interaction produces the stated result. Such tests establish functional conformance only to the extent that the acceptance criteria formalise the intended behaviour. Acceptance testing can establish conformance to a stated requirement; it cannot establish that the requirement adequately represents the scholarly practice from which it was derived.

A domain-specific comparison is provided by Strutz’s framework for evaluating LLM-generated TEI encodings of historical correspondence. The framework separates syntactic validity, source fidelity, schema compliance, structural fidelity, and semantic recognition instead of treating valid XML as sufficient evidence of an adequate encoding (Strutz 2026). Its accompanying benchmark contains 100 plain-text letter transcriptions, manually curated TEI reference documents, and 400 outputs generated by four LLMs across five prompt scenarios, together with dimension-specific evaluation results (Strutz 2025). Parsing and schema validation correspond to deterministic validation; textual, structural, and semantic comparisons provide assessment evidence; and potentially defensible divergences from a reference encoding still require expert verification. The framework therefore offers an external, domain-specific example of scalable checking that does not transfer scholarly authority to the model.

Design conformance and interpretation remain with the Critical Expert. The existence of a working map does not establish that its spatial representation is appropriate. The presence of a network edge does not establish that the underlying historical relation has been interpreted correctly. Verification is therefore not an inefficiency left behind after automation. It is the scholarly labour through which an artefact becomes acceptable.

The boundary between these forms of checking is not fixed. Where the Critical Expert has verified outputs and their rules can be formalised, part of that verification work is converted into deterministic validation, as when accepted encoding decisions become a schema against which subsequent generation is checked. The conversion never runs in the opposite direction: no accumulation of passed checks establishes scholarly adequacy.

The term *Critical Expert in the Loop* distinguishes this role from an unspecified human checkpoint. It requires competence in the relevant domain and awareness of LLM-specific failure modes. Sycophancy may lead a model to reproduce an assumption embedded in a request rather than challenge it (Sharma et al. 2024). Confabulation may fill a gap in the available material with a plausible but false continuation. The jagged frontier of model capability means that apparently similar tasks may differ substantially in reliability, and the boundary cannot be inferred from surface difficulty alone (Dell’Acqua et al. 2023). Competence therefore develops through work with the particular systems, data, and task types involved.

### Iteration, acceptance, and write-back

The Critical Expert’s responsibility extends beyond checking generated content. It includes evaluating the possibility space that the system did not produce: alternatives not explored, categories not questioned, inherited conventions reproduced, and absences hidden by the interface. In Exploration, the expert judges proposals. In Distillation, they decide which project knowledge must remain available. In Implementation, they verify the artefact and determine whether corrections belong in the specification, the assumptions about the data, the source preparation, or the implementation instructions.

The defining loop runs from Implementation back into the knowledge base. If an artefact is wrong because a requirement was absent, a category was underspecified, or a design instruction allowed an unwarranted interpretation, the persistent correction belongs in the specification rather than only in the code. The artefact is then regenerated or revised from the corrected documents. Unexpected data characteristics, performance constraints, improved formulations, and revised research questions are likewise written back. Re-entry may go deeper than Distillation: a finding that the data affords less or more than assumed returns the work to Exploration, while a newly received source, schema, or editorial guideline returns it to Preparation.

Implementation proceeds most reliably in inspectable milestones. Each milestone should add a bounded increment small enough for the researcher to evaluate before the next is built. A first pass may deliberately prioritise seeing an artefact run, but any deferred inspection must be completed before the artefact is accepted, used as evidence, published, or handed over.

An iteration ends when the artefact and the maintained project knowledge form a coherent versioned state that satisfies the applicable acceptance and verification criteria. That state constitutes a promptotype. Acceptance is purpose-specific: the same state may be sufficient for an exploratory demonstration, an internal workflow, a proposal-stage model, or publication only under different criteria. Acceptance therefore closes an iteration without claiming that the project, data, or artefact has become final.

Figure 4 distinguishes the internal cycles of one iteration from the succession of accepted promptotypes. New findings, sources, requirements, or improved checks are written back and initiate another iteration, which may produce a new promptotype.

![Three versioned promptotypes connected by write-back and renewed iterations.](assets/figures/figure-4-versioned-promptotypes.png)

**Figure 4.** Versioned promptotypes across successive iterations. Each accepted Promptotyping iteration yields an identifiable versioned state that connects maintained project knowledge and the corresponding artefact. Later findings initiate renewed work and may result in another promptotype.

The version identifier is methodologically important, but Promptotyping is not tied to GitHub, release objects, or Semantic Versioning. An accepted state must be identifiable well enough that its project knowledge, artefact, research-data reference, and grounds of acceptance can be recovered together. A tag, release, or archived snapshot is a strong implementation of this requirement, but it is not constitutive. In the documented repositories, identifiable states may instead be established through a commit SHA linked to a dated journal entry, a version field in document frontmatter, a numbered and dated decision record, or a dated iteration designation. These mechanisms are weaker than an archived release when they do not freeze all components, but they can still identify the state to which acceptance refers. The labels `v0.0.1`, `v0.0.2`, and `v0.1.0` are therefore illustrative rather than prescribed. The method recommends an explicit release or snapshot where feasible and requires at minimum a durable, unambiguous reference to the accepted state.

### Organisation and responsibility

Two kinds of judgement are combined in the Critical Expert role: scholarly judgement about whether the artefact represents the sources and addresses the research question, and agentic-engineering judgement about how the work is organised, tested, and steered. They need not be held by one person; Section 3.4 develops this distinction and its consequences for the transferability of the method.

The method can operate with one researcher and one agent instance or with several differentiated agents. The single-agent mode is the default and accounts for most of the documented practice discussed in this paper. In more complex projects, a lead agent may coordinate sub-agents responsible for separate components, data domains, or verification tasks. Permissions should follow the principle of least privilege: each agent receives only the tools, files, and authority required for its task (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975). This limits the consequences of error and narrows what later review must trace.

Multi-agent organisation does not remove the Critical Expert’s responsibility. It increases the need for explicit role definitions, knowledge boundaries, handovers, and verification. The researcher’s methodological task shifts from steering a single executing instance towards designing a small work organisation. The cognitive and auditing costs of that mode limit its usefulness and are discussed in Section 5.

The SZD pipeline shows the division of labour that these arrangements establish across the phases. The scholar determines the purpose of the transcription, models the source types, specifies editorial and verification rules, and authorises accepted output. The agent implements pipelines, interfaces, transformations, and checks within those specifications. The claim is not that generative systems are incapable of proposing models, specifications, or assessments. It is that responsibility for scholarly decisions remains with the persons accountable for the research.

# 3. Epistemic and Methodological Implications

Promptotyping does not automate a neutral passage from research data to software. No such neutral passage exists: research data are modelled representations, and every computational form derived from them selects, organises, and operationalises particular distinctions. Promptotyping instead amplifies this work by externalising parts of the translation between data, scholarly intent, and implementation into maintained documents, without removing the need for scholarly judgement.

This chapter examines what follows from that condition, moving from the artefact to the people and obligations that remain attached to it. Section 3.1 treats generated artefacts as scholarly arguments. Section 3.2 examines what semantically explicit research data contribute to this work and what they leave open. Section 3.3 characterises Promptotyping Documents as conceptual models mediating between data, scholarly intent, and implementation. Section 3.4 develops the claim that the method amplifies rather than replaces competence and distinguishes the judgements this involves. Section 3.5 distinguishes the acceptance of a promptotype from its publication and the additional obligations that publication creates, and Section 3.6 establishes what can and cannot be reconstructed from a documented Promptotyping project.

## 3.1 Research Artefacts as Scholarly Arguments

Exploration and building have long been recognised as forms of inquiry within the digital humanities. Generous interfaces expose collections and support investigation before a researcher knows precisely what to search for (Whitelaw 2015). Visualisation may likewise be valuable as a process of provisional construction rather than only as the production of a stable final representation (Hinrichs, Forlini, and Moynihan 2019). Prototypes can function as experiments, provocations, and arguments because their design decisions make claims about the material they organise (Galey and Ruecker 2010).

A digital research artefact is therefore not a transparent container through which findings are merely displayed. Selecting which entities become nodes, assigning direction to an edge, aggregating observations into a category, arranging events on a timeline, or representing uncertainty through a particular visual form are interpretative decisions. They determine which relations become visible, which comparisons become possible, and which absences recede from view.

LLM-based generation does not suspend this problem. A generated bar chart, timeline, map, dashboard, or network can carry the same assumptions as a manually implemented one. Drucker’s critique of inherited visual conventions therefore remains applicable: familiar graphical forms may imply precision, completeness, comparability, directionality, or categorical stability that humanistic data do not warrant (Drucker 2014).

Promptotyping responds by making representational decisions part of the maintained project description. As set out in Sections 2.2 and 2.3, these decisions may concern uncertainty, missingness, directionality, aggregation, or contested classifications. Their significance here is epistemic: once such assumptions are stated in a design document, they become attributable scholarly claims rather than incidental consequences of a generated implementation.

The documents do not guarantee that an artefact conforms to those claims. They make the grounds for evaluating it explicit. A scholar can compare a visual representation with the documented design, the data model, and the sources, determine whether an error lies in the implementation or in the specification, and revise the relevant layer. The artefact and the documents can therefore be criticised together.

Digital tool criticism asks what a tool does to its material, which assumptions it embeds, and whether it is appropriate for the inquiry in which it is used (van Es, Wieringa, and Schäfer 2018; Koolen, van Gorp, and van Ossenbruggen 2019). Promptotyping provides this criticism with a project-level object: the versioned description from which the artefact was derived and against which its representational decisions can be examined.

A technically adequate implementation is not necessarily an adequate resolution of the underlying scholarly problem. A functioning interface may still rest on inadequate categories, unsupported mappings, or an inappropriate model of uncertainty. The artefact matters because it makes research data addressable in a particular way; its scholarly adequacy depends on the modelling, contextualisation, and verification that precede and accompany it.

This also limits the claim that building is itself research. Implementation becomes epistemically productive when it reveals characteristics of the data, consequences of a model, or alternatives for representing a research problem. A generated artefact is not automatically a scholarly contribution merely because it exists. Its research value lies in what can be learned, argued, and verified through building it, and the verification arrangements of Section 2.4 provide the criteria through which that value is established rather than asserted.

## 3.2 Semantic Explicitness and the Limits of Context

Research data are representations used as the basis for scholarly claims. Their status depends not only on their contents but on the preparation, contextualisation, provenance, and durability through which findings can be examined and research processes reproduced or reused. In the humanities, they are selectively constructed, machine-actionable abstractions of research objects rather than neutral records of them (Owens 2011; Posner 2015). Their production is itself interpretative modelling.

Promptotyping depends on this prior work. A model may generate code from a CSV file without knowing why its columns were selected, what an empty field means, which categories are interpretative, or what was excluded when the table was constructed. More semantically explicit formats can make some of these distinctions machine-addressable. TEI, LIDO, CMIF, PAGE XML, RDF, and project-specific ontologies can represent entities, relations, constraints, provenance, controlled vocabularies, and uncertainty.

When such structures are represented explicitly, the project documents can refer to and summarise them instead of reconstructing them from isolated examples or informal explanation. This does not mean that a formal data model contains the complete research context. It means that parts of the structural and semantic work have already been made available in a form that both deterministic software and the Promptotyping context layer can use.

The M³GIM project illustrates this distinction. Its project vocabulary defines classes, properties, domains, ranges, typed date relations, controlled values, and alignments with the archival standard on which the data model builds. These structures could be carried into a domain document with comparatively little interpretative reconstruction. The document could identify the class hierarchy, permitted relations, and value structures by drawing directly on the vocabulary.

The vocabulary did not, however, express the complete scholarly significance of the resulting data. It did not by itself state the research question, describe the empirical shape of the project graph, identify deliberate absences, or establish the reliability of an individual entry. Some meanings remained dependent on prose annotations and project-specific explanation. Formalising the vocabulary also exposed modelling problems, including role values that became difficult to distinguish once the hierarchical context of the source had been removed.

The example shows that semantic explicitness changes rather than eliminates the work of contextualisation. Standards and ontologies can carry structural and terminological commitments. The Promptotyping Documents must still explain how those commitments relate to the research question, which limitations matter for the intended artefact, and how particular observations should be interpreted.

Read from the perspective of Context Engineering, this suggests an additional benefit of FAIR preparation (Wilkinson et al. 2016). Data made interoperable and reusable through explicit structures and shared vocabularies provide more stable points of reference for subsequent descriptions and implementations. This is an inference from the Promptotyping cases rather than a claim contained in the FAIR principles themselves.

Semantic richness also creates a contrary technical pressure. Richly encoded XML and RDF serialisations may require substantially more context than a compact table or project-specific summary, and, as Section 2.3 establishes, nominal context capacity does not ensure that every part of a long context will be used with equal reliability. Semantic explicitness and direct suitability for an LLM context are therefore not the same property.

Promptotyping separates the source, contextual, and operational functions of the material. The complete research data remain available to the generated program. The agent receives the task-relevant account of their structure and meaning developed through Distillation. It then produces parsers, transformations, queries, tests, and rendering logic that operate on the complete data outside the model context. The compact arrangement stated in Section 2.3 follows from this distinction: the LLM reads about the data and writes code that reads the data. The description does not replace the data, and the data do not replace the description.

Distillation therefore does not reverse the explication achieved through semantic modelling. It reorganises that work for a different use. Classes, relations, constraints, exceptions, and uncertainties can be selected for the context precisely because they have already been identified in the data model, schema, editorial guidelines, or project documentation. The intellectual investment in making knowledge explicit can survive changes in its carrier.

Seen historically, this arrangement recalls the Semantic Web vision of agents operating over semantically described information (Berners-Lee, Hendler, and Lassila 2001). The resemblance is genealogical rather than technically equivalent. Promptotyping does not depend on general-purpose formal inference over a complete graph. It uses explicit semantics to construct a task-specific context from which an agent generates deterministic operations over the underlying data.

## 3.3 Documents as Conceptual Models and Mediating Structures

Digital humanities projects have traditionally addressed the distance between scholarly requirements and technical implementation through collaboration. Domain specialists interpret sources and articulate research questions; developers or Research Software Engineers translate these requirements into data structures, workflows, and interfaces. This translation is not a one-directional transfer of complete information. It is a negotiation across vocabularies, assumptions, practices, and standards of adequacy.

Promptotyping gives part of this negotiation a persistent written form. A data document can be inspected by the scholar responsible for the account of the sources and used by an agent deriving parsing or transformation logic from it. A requirements document expressed through user stories is simultaneously a statement about scholarly practice and an implementable specification. A design document records how the project intends particular distinctions to become visible. An action document translates the maintained project description into constraints on agent behaviour.

The documents do not dissolve differences between scholarly and technical judgement. They provide shared objects through which these judgements can be articulated, compared, revised, and handed over. Their authority does not reside in the files themselves. It arises from the accountable engagement of the researchers and technical contributors who maintain them, challenge their assumptions, and decide which formulations are accepted as the current basis for implementation.

This function does not depend exclusively on generative tooling. The knowledge base can also support communication and handover to a human developer or Research Software Engineer. In an agentic workflow, however, the documents carry an additional operational load because they are used directly as context for implementation.

Their persistence follows from this double function. A conversation with an LLM may contain a relevant explanation, but it does not necessarily leave a stable object that another scholar, developer, or agent instance can inspect. A versioned document can be read by the person responsible for its claims, used during implementation, revised when the project’s understanding changes, and cited when a resulting decision is discussed.

Promptotyping Documents can therefore be understood as conceptual models of a project (Mayr and Thalheim 2021). They represent its sources, domain concepts, purposes, intended operations, and design decisions in natural language structured by the vocabulary of scholarly and technical communities. Their semi-formality balances practical communication with residual ambiguity.

Stachowiak’s general theory of models clarifies this document layer (Stachowiak 1973). The documents represent originals such as source structures, research practices, requirements, and intended operations. They abbreviate them by retaining the attributes relevant to their purpose, and they are pragmatic because they are created for particular addressees, periods, and uses.

Abbreviation provides the theoretical basis for Distillation independently of technical context limits. Omitting information that does not contribute to a particular purpose is constitutive of modelling rather than merely an accommodation to a token budget. Pragmatism explains why the same project may require different documents or different levels of detail when its question, artefact, agent, or human addressee changes.

The documents constrain implementation without determining a unique result. Natural-language specifications retain ambiguity, and different agent runs may realise them differently. The resulting artefact must therefore be inspected against the knowledge base, the research data, and the source material rather than treated as correct because it was generated from a detailed description.

Knowledge Engineering defines an ontology as an explicit specification of a conceptualisation (Gruber 1993). Promptotyping Documents are not ontologies and do not claim the formal semantics of OWL or RDF. They are structurally related in that they provide an explicit, project-specific account of a conceptualisation at the semi-formal end of the representational spectrum. Their function is not to replace the formal data model but to connect it with research practices, implementation requirements, and verification criteria that the formal model does not fully express.

The demarcation can be stated precisely. Promptotyping Documents support no formal inference, no automated consistency checking, and no validation in the sense of a reasoner or schema. Their claims are checked by readers, agents, and the verification arrangements of the project, not by a formal semantics. This limitation is accepted deliberately. Formalising the documents further would narrow the circle of contributors able to read, challenge, and maintain them, and it would displace the negotiation between scholarly and technical judgement that the documents exist to support. The residual ambiguity of semi-formal description is therefore not only a deficiency to be engineered away. It marks the point at which interpretation remains open and verification remains necessary.

Generative implementation reduces the manual formalisation required between this semi-formal project model and a running system. Schemas, queries, transformations, tests, and interface code can be produced from the knowledge base without every step first being translated manually into a separate formal specification. The remaining ambiguity and variation explain why verification is constitutive of the method rather than a final optional check.

## 3.4 Amplification, Distributed Authority, and the Limits of Externalisation

A hybrid scholar-developer combines domain responsibility for the research material with sufficient technical competence to design, inspect, and revise its implementation. The method was developed from this position. That creates a limitation in the experience reported here, since many early cases succeeded under the direction of one person who knew the data, the research context, and the generated code. It also identifies the competence that Promptotyping attempts to externalise in part.

*Amplification* refers here to increasing the range, continuity, and reusability of an existing competence rather than replacing that competence. Promptotyping amplifies translation work by preserving parts of it in the knowledge base. A requirement that would otherwise need to be explained repeatedly can guide several implementation sessions. A decision about data semantics can be reused across agents and artefacts. A design rationale can survive an interruption, be inspected by another scholar, or support a handover to a developer. Experience with recurrent model failures can be incorporated into action and verification documents rather than remaining tacit knowledge held by one operator.

This externalisation is necessarily incomplete. Scholarly judgement may depend on familiarity with a corpus, sensitivity to an exceptional source, awareness of disciplinary debates, or recognition that an apparently plausible output excludes a relevant possibility. The knowledge base contains the portion of this understanding that has been articulated and maintained. Its limits become visible when implementation produces a question or failure for which the documents contain no adequate answer.

Promptotyping should therefore be understood as amplifying rather than automating the translation capacity of a hybrid practitioner. Hybrid practitioners can apply their competence across more implementation steps because agents perform part of the formalisation and coding. The teaching cases examined in Section 4 indicate whether non-hybrid scholars can use the same structure to gain access to a bounded part of technical implementation. They do not establish that the underlying competence has become unnecessary.

Authority is distributed among people, sources, documents, and formal procedures, but not in the same sense. Research data and source materials provide the basis against which scholarly claims are examined. Scholars remain responsible for interpretation, contextualisation, and acceptance. Developers and Research Software Engineers remain responsible for technical decisions within their professional remit. The knowledge base provides the maintained reference through which these responsibilities are articulated and related. Its practical authority comes from the ongoing engagement of the people who work with it, not from the documents independently of that engagement. Deterministic checks decide only questions that have been formalised as rules, while agentic review can extend comparison and testing without authorising a claim whose validity depends on domain judgement.

The distinction between scholarly and agentic-engineering judgement follows from this distribution. Scholarly judgement determines whether an artefact represents the sources appropriately, addresses the research question, and carries the qualifications needed for interpretation. Agentic-engineering judgement determines how work is decomposed, which tools and tests are appropriate, how permissions are bounded, and how a failure should be formulated so that an agent can act on it.

The two judgements need not be held by one person. In the Notker case, the commissioning scholar exercised scholarly judgement: he supplied the internally annotated source document, decided how textual layers, language changes, and sigla were to be modelled, and verified generated TEI output against his editorial intentions. Agentic-engineering judgement lay with the author, who decomposed the workflow, directed the agentic implementation, and translated accepted editorial decisions into implementable instructions and deterministic checks where this was possible. The scholar’s judgement remained decisive where adequacy could not be expressed as rules, including the examination of the resulting edition interface against the intended editorial model.

Where the judgements are distributed in this way, the knowledge base mediates between them. Templates and instructions can support agentic-engineering judgement, but they cannot substitute fully for the ability to diagnose technical behaviour, evaluate a workflow, or formulate an actionable correction. The boundary of the method therefore depends not only on whether a scholar can state a requirement, but also on whether the resulting implementation can be inspected and steered responsibly.

The Critical Expert retains authority where acceptance depends on scholarly judgement. This does not mean that generative systems cannot propose interpretations, models, specifications, or assessments. It means that producing a plausible proposal and taking responsibility for its scholarly validity are different acts.

The same principle applies to authorship. Tools may contribute formulations, code, mappings, designs, and evaluations, but the persons presenting an artefact remain accountable for the scholarly claims it carries. Responsibility follows from the act of acceptance and publication, not from exclusive manual production.

Promptotyping does not claim that the translation between research data, scholarly intent, and implementation can be fully externalised. It externalises the part that can be articulated, versioned, and operationalised, and thereby extends its reach across implementations, sessions, and collaborators. What cannot be fully externalised remains visible at the points where scholars and technical contributors must interpret, select, contextualise, decide, and verify.

## 3.5 Promptotypes, Acceptance, and Publication

A promptotype marks an accepted iteration state, not the final completion of a project. Its acceptance is relative to an intended purpose and to the criteria established for that purpose. A promptotype may be adequate for exploring what the data supports, examining the consequences of a modelling decision, demonstrating a proposed edition model, or operating an internal workflow without yet being suitable for publication or sustained third-party use.

This distinction separates **iteration acceptance** from **publication**. Iteration acceptance establishes that the maintained project knowledge, artefact, and documented basis of acceptance form a coherent versioned state. Publication places that state, or an artefact derived from it, into a scholarly record and creates additional obligations. Readers then require a stable reference, accessible data where legally and ethically possible, an account of provenance, and sufficient documentation to understand the decisions embodied in the artefact. Static publication and versioned documents support these requirements, but archived releases, persistent identifiers, citation metadata, licensing, accessibility, and preservation remain separate publication tasks.

A published digital research artefact should identify the promptotype from which it was released. The promptotype may be represented through a repository tag, release, archived deposit, or another persistent snapshot. Publication does not make the underlying project knowledge final: corrections, new sources, or revised interpretations may initiate another Promptotyping iteration and produce a later promptotype. What publication changes is not the possibility of revision but the obligation to preserve and identify the released state.

The same logic applies to internal use and handover. An accepted promptotype can remain unpublished, support communication among project partners, or provide a structured package for a human developer or Research Software Engineer. The status of a promptotype must therefore be stated explicitly: accepted for which purpose, against which criteria, by whom, and whether it has been published or transferred into maintained software.

## 3.6 Reconstructability and Model Dependence

Running the same Promptotyping Documents again does not guarantee an identical implementation. LLM outputs are non-deterministic, model versions change, and proprietary systems may be updated without preserving earlier behaviour.

*Reconstructability* refers here to the ability to trace an accepted promptotype and any published artefact derived from it back through its code, documents, decisions, source-data reference, and verification record rather than to reproduce an identical implementation through renewed generation.

What can be reconstructed is the documented project logic: the data state used, the requirements established, the design decisions taken, the process recorded in the journal, the models and tools disclosed, the deterministic transformations applied to the data, and the basis on which the iteration state was accepted. Git history records changes to documents and code, while tests and generated reports can make parts of the data-processing path repeatable. A tag, release, or archived snapshot can identify the particular combination of documents, code, artefact, and references that constituted a promptotype.

The deterministic layer is especially important. Once accepted code, dependencies, and source data are versioned, deterministic transformations can in principle be re-executed independently of the non-deterministic process that generated the code. Generated documents derived from source data can likewise be refreshed through named commands. The reconstructable object is therefore the versioned relation among source data, maintained documents, accepted code, artefact, process record, and provenance information.

This position does not remove dependence on commercial frontier models. Promptotyping currently relies on systems whose weights, training data, updates, and long-term availability are outside the researcher’s control. Re-running an old project may therefore require a different model and produce another implementation. The knowledge base adds a layer of reusability alongside the code by preserving the project logic from which a new implementation can be derived. A later implementation should be treated as a new iteration rather than silently identified with the earlier promptotype.

# 4. Promptotyping in Practice: Artefact Forms and Documented Projects

Promptotyping can create and revise many kinds of digital research artefact, including data descriptions, models, mappings, scripts, processing pipelines, verification environments, editions, interfaces, and publications. This chapter presents the recurrent browser-based artefact forms produced across the documented projects and the projects themselves, conducted between 2023 and 2026. The projects are not presented as controlled validation of the method. They differ in data, duration, model capability, scholarly depth, technical complexity, and degree of documentation. Most were conducted by the same hybrid scholar-developer, and failures were not recorded through a systematic protocol. They provide the practical basis from which the recurring phases, document functions, interface types, and verification practices were identified, and they allow the method's claims, limits, and development to be discussed against concrete cases.

## 4.1 Self-Contained Static Artefacts

A generic tool serves multiple projects at the level of structure they share. A project-specific digital research artefact is instead bound to the distinctions represented in one project’s data model, requirements, and research context. Its scholarly function derives from this binding rather than from technical novelty.

Across the documented projects, self-contained static web artefacts became the preferred implementation form where the research purpose did not require server-side processing or shared state. They consist of HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and research data embedded in the artefact or loaded from flat files. They can be deployed on a static host or opened locally without server-side execution. The form keeps the path from documents to implementation comparatively inspectable, requires little infrastructure, and corresponds to the limited maintenance resources available to many individual researchers and small projects.

This preference aligns with approaches to minimal and sustainable computing. Risam and Gil (2022) argue for technical forms whose resource requirements and dependencies remain proportionate to their scholarly purposes. The Endings Project likewise identifies reduced technological complexity and minimal dependency chains as important conditions for the longevity of digital scholarship (Holmes and Takeda 2023). Static publication has also been adopted in digital scholarly editing. Andorfer (2026), for example, publishes TEI-based editions as pre-rendered static pages and deliberately avoids an extensive JavaScript build stack. Promptotyping reaches a similar target form through agentic generation rather than deterministic XSLT transformation.

The static artefact also resembles a research compendium in bringing data, documentation, code, and output together within a versioned repository (Marwick, Boettiger, and Mullen 2018). The comparison is not exact: a promptotype may be generated non-deterministically from documents, while the transformations operating on its data should remain deterministic. Nevertheless, both arrangements make the relation between source material, method, and output available for inspection.

The technical defaults follow from this aim:

- Vanilla JavaScript is preferred where it can implement the required behaviour without an additional framework or build process.  
- External libraries are used selectively and, where feasible, stored locally with the artefact.  
- Computationally expensive processing is performed upstream, and the artefact receives derived data suitable for client-side use.  
- Runtime dependencies on external APIs or services are avoided where the artefact can fulfil its research purpose without them.  
- Each published artefact should include a provenance declaration identifying the promptotype, knowledge base, data state, models, tools, and verification procedures involved in its production.

These are defaults rather than defining conditions of Promptotyping. A project may require a generated processing pipeline upstream of the interface. A document may be rendered deterministically from source data. Earlier promptotypes took the form of notebooks or configured language-model environments rather than web applications. What these cases share is not a particular frontend technology, but derivation from a maintained knowledge base and verification against the research material and requirements.

The relation to FAIR for Research Software is mixed (Chue Hong et al. 2022). Where both artefact and repository can be published, static deployment supports accessibility without requiring proprietary runtime infrastructure. The knowledge base, journal, provenance declaration, and repository history can provide detailed information about how and why the artefact was produced. Findability does not follow automatically. Persistent identifiers, archived releases, citation metadata, and formal publication remain additional responsibilities. Promptotyping can prepare an artefact for publication, but it does not itself complete that publication work.

The limits of the static form are part of its definition. Client-side processing is constrained by browser memory, data volume, and data representation. Large transformations, model inference, OCR, HTR, and computationally expensive aggregation normally remain upstream in generated pipelines, and static artefacts lack the shared server-side state required for simultaneous multi-user editing, institutional authentication, central permission management, or continuously updated databases. Pre-rendering, segmentation, indexing, and precomputation can extend the range of static publication, but they do not remove the limit.

The handover point is reached when the obligations attached to the artefact change. Relevant conditions include continuing maintenance beyond the original project, external users with an expectation of support, shared or sensitive data, multi-user operation, security requirements, institutional deployment, and commitments to availability. These conditions may arise independently of one another and cannot be reduced to a single technical threshold. Once such obligations arise, the standards and responsibilities of Research Software Engineering apply according to what the artefact has become, irrespective of whether its first implementation was produced through Promptotyping. The knowledge base remains useful at this boundary. It provides a data description, requirements, acceptance criteria, design rationale, process record, and provenance account that can support a structured handover to professional development.

## 4.2 An Epistemic Typology of Interfaces

The browser-based artefacts are classified here by the epistemic function they support rather than by their data format or visualisation technique. TaDiRAH classifies research activities rather than the digital research artefacts through which those activities are conducted (Borek et al. 2021). The present typology complements that vocabulary by asking what role an interface plays in producing, examining, correcting, or assessing scholarly knowledge.

Four recurrent interface types and one provisional extension can be distinguished across the documented projects.

**Verification Interfaces** expose intermediate or final outputs for comparison with their sources or with outputs produced through alternative procedures. They introduce review points into processing pipelines so that errors can be identified before they propagate into later stages. Examples include OCR and HTR comparison environments, layout overlays, and viewers that place generated transcriptions beside facsimiles.

**Exploration Interfaces** make existing structured research data investigable through filters, linked views, visualisations, and alternative forms of navigation. Their purpose is not only to retrieve known items but to reveal patterns, absences, concentrations, and possible relations. They operationalise the exploratory function associated with generous interfaces and coordinated multiple views (Whitelaw 2015; Roberts 2007; Windhager et al. 2019).

**Edition Interfaces** render scholarly editions and connect encoded texts with facsimiles, apparatus, translations, annotations, or editorial correction. Interface decisions in this context are themselves editorial decisions because they determine how textual layers, uncertainty, variants, and scholarly interventions become accessible (Pierazzo 2015).

**Capture Interfaces** support structured data input, annotation, correction, or metadata creation. Because the available fields and relations define what can be entered, these interfaces also test the adequacy of the underlying data model. Difficulties encountered during capture can therefore lead to revisions of the schema, vocabulary, or modelling assumptions.

**Audit Interfaces** expose the process through which results were produced. They may show alternative assessments, disagreements, processing stages, statistics, provenance, or the relation between an output and the decisions that shaped it. Their object is not only the research data or result but the inspectability of the research process itself.

The first four types recur across several projects. Audit Interfaces are represented by one case and should therefore be treated as a provisional extension of the typology.

The categories are not mutually exclusive. A processing project may combine Verification Interfaces at several stages, an Edition Interface for reading, and Capture functions for scholarly correction. M³GIM combines exploration with structured input, while the ZBZ pipeline combines stage-specific verification with an edition reader. The typology identifies the primary epistemic functions an artefact supports without requiring every artefact to belong to only one category.

The categories also combine primary epistemic functions with established scholarly artefact forms. Edition Interfaces are retained as a separate type because the editorial relation between encoded text, apparatus, facsimile, intervention, and reader constitutes a distinct design and verification regime.

This functional classification explains why a single general-purpose dashboard cannot serve every research perspective equally well. The same TEI or RDF dataset may require an Exploration Interface, an Edition Interface, a Verification Interface, or a combination of them depending on the research question and workflow. The data format does not determine the artefact type. The epistemic activity established through requirements engineering does.

The typology describes what an interface enables, not when in the project lifecycle it is produced. The same interface type may serve different temporal functions. An Edition Interface may publish a completed scholarly edition or make a proposed editorial model concrete during the preparation of a funding application. An Exploration or Capture Interface may operate on a stable corpus or be generated from the first available records while data collection and modelling are still in progress. Epistemic function and project maturity should therefore be treated as separate dimensions.

## 4.3 Principle of Presentation

A project is included in the inventory when Promptotyping was applied across the project rather than only to an isolated coding task and when the work left a repository containing a meaningful combination of documents, data, code, artefacts, or process records. Public access is not a condition of inclusion because several projects remain subject to partner or data restrictions. Teaching demonstrations without a project repository are discussed separately.

The inventory documents application across different materials and project forms; it does not support comparative claims about efficacy.

Repository-wide claims follow an evidence rule derived from the audit of the project corpus. They are not inferred from one repository, one directory convention, or one filename pattern. Test infrastructure is reconstructed from runner entry points such as package scripts, test configuration, dedicated runners, and continuous-integration workflows, and then traced to the files they execute. Provenance is distinguished at two levels: declarations embedded in the delivered artefact and model or tool declarations in document frontmatter. A negative finding names the inspection procedure and remains a statement about what was not found under that procedure.

The companion repository contains the current manuscript, the method specification, document templates, records of selected claim checks, and links to the relevant public project repositories. It provides a versioned record of the paper’s own development without introducing a separate methodological architecture.

## 4.5 Worked Cases by Epistemic Function

The following cases are presented through a common structure: source data, documents, artefact, epistemic and project contribution, and limits. The structure supports comparison while keeping the claims attached to concrete project conditions.

#### Verification: ZBZ OCR/TEI Pipeline

- **Source data:** PDF scans of multilingual estate material held by the Zentralbibliothek Zürich and spanning several document genres.  
- **Documents:** A knowledge base describing the pipeline stage by stage, together with the generated and maintained Python codebase.  
- **Artefact:** Verification Interfaces for comparing OCR outputs and inspecting layout, followed by an Edition Interface for reading the processed material.  
- **Epistemic and project contribution:** The interfaces create defined review points intended to prevent errors from propagating unchecked through the pipeline. Outputs were handed to the partner institution with their verification state explicitly marked.  
- **Limits:** The project approaches infrastructure scale. Although its interfaces remain client-side, the staged pipeline, processing code, data management, and verification burden exceed the lightweight form of smaller promptotypes.

#### Exploration: CorrespExplorer

- **Source data:** CMIF-encoded correspondence metadata from the correspSearch aggregation.  
- **Documents:** User stories, a data and design description, a phase-based journal, and a browser-based test suite.  
- **Artefact:** Twelve coordinated visualisation views over the correspondence metadata.  
- **Epistemic and project contribution:** The project generated a range of possible views that were subsequently evaluated and reduced against domain criteria. The interface made the same correspondence data addressable through temporal, geographical, social, and quantitative perspectives.  
- **Limits:** Many initial views reproduced established conventions of correspondence visualisation. Decisions to retain, revise, or reject those conventions depended heavily on the design document and expert review. The journal also records qualitative context degradation during longer implementation sessions, but the effect was not systematically measured.

#### Edition: Notker Psalm Commentary

- **Source data:** A sample from Notker III of St. Gallen’s Old High German Psalm commentary prepared for the development of a research proposal. The material combines Latin psalm text, Old High German translation, and exegetical commentary within the same textual flow.  
- **Documents:** Editorial guidelines describing the intended encoding of textual phenomena, functional layers, language changes, source references, and marginal sigla. TEI-XML was specified as the canonical encoded source from which the interface representation would be derived.  
- **Artefact:** A proposal-stage Edition Interface with independently toggleable textual layers, a modern German translation, IIIF facsimile access, a patristic source apparatus, a comparison of psalm witnesses, and a parallel textual tradition.  
- **Epistemic and project contribution:** The promptotype made the proposed editorial model visible and operable before the larger project existed. It allowed the intended relations between transcription, translation, commentary, apparatus, facsimile, and parallel traditions to be examined in a concrete interface rather than described only in the application text. The artefact thereby served both as an exploratory scholarly model and as a demonstrator for the funding proposal. It also exposed editorial questions that would have remained less visible in a purely textual project description. One example concerns the siglum `R`, whose interpretation may depend on whether it occurs beside a psalm citation or within a commentary segment.  
- **Limits:** The artefact represents a proposed editorial approach rather than the result of a completed edition project. Its data basis is deliberately limited, and several editorial decisions require confirmation by the commissioning scholar. The interface demonstrates what the proposed model could support; it does not establish that the model is complete or philologically verified across the corpus.

#### Exploration and Capture: M³GIM

- **Source data:** Archival information represented in RiC-O and JSON-LD. Data capture, vocabulary development, and modelling proceeded in parallel rather than beginning from a completed and stable dataset.  
- **Documents:** A data and domain description, vocabulary documentation, session journal, and decision register recording modelling choices, unresolved questions, and changes made during data capture and interface development.  
- **Artefact:** An archive explorer with eight functional areas and structured data-entry capabilities. Early versions of the interface were generated rapidly from the first available project data and revised as further records and modelling requirements emerged.  
- **Epistemic and project contribution:** The interface was not added after data collection and modelling had been completed. It developed alongside them. Generating a usable interface from the first recorded data made the emerging structure of the project visible at an early stage and provided a shared object through which project participants could discuss entities, relations, controlled values, navigation, and capture requirements. Problems that remained abstract in vocabulary or schema discussions became concrete when records had to be displayed, filtered, connected, or entered. Conversely, interface proposals prompted questions about distinctions not yet represented in the data. The cycle ran in both directions: the current data model shaped the generated interface, while interaction with the interface informed further data capture and modelling decisions.  
- **Limits:** Rapid generation can make an early and incomplete model appear more settled than it is. Interface convenience may also encourage changes that solve a local presentation or capture problem without improving the conceptual model. The journal and decision register make these changes traceable, but responsibility for distinguishing justified model refinement from interface-driven model drift remains with the project team.

Audit remains outside the worked cases because it is represented by only one project and has not yet recurred sufficiently to support analysis at the same level as the other types.

## 4.6 Teaching and Collaboration Experiences

Whether Promptotyping can be used by scholars who do not combine domain and development expertise remains open. Teaching and collaboration experiences provide preliminary observations about communicability rather than independent validation.

At a full-day AI winter school for digital humanities researchers in February 2026, participants worked through the four phases with an agentic coding tool, developed data descriptions and user stories, and produced provisional interfaces within the available workshop time. A data-steward training module used the same document types to examine the relation between FAIR data preparation and generated artefacts. A workshop of the Museumsbund Österreich introduced the path from collection data through project documents to functional interfaces. Additional project collaborations used one-pagers, workshops, and coaching to support domain experts in developing prototypes for research and administrative workflows.

These experiences indicate that the phases and document types can be communicated and followed in bounded teaching settings. They do not establish that participants can independently produce, maintain, and verify artefacts in their subsequent research. Participant outputs are not included in the public project corpus, and the teaching observations were not collected through a common evaluation protocol.

Collaboration within documented projects provides a second form of experience. In the Notker case, the commissioning scholar retained authority over philological decisions that the editorial guidelines could not settle. In the ZBZ pipeline, processed material was transferred to the partner institution with verification states made explicit. These cases illustrate how scholarly specification and verification can remain with domain experts while implementation proceeds through agentic tooling.

Documented third-party continuation is currently limited to the coOCR-HTR fork. More independent use is needed before claims about transfer beyond the originating practitioner can be made.

## 4.7 Cross-Case Observations

The repository audit also corrected an initially over-uniform account of project practice. Branching and merging vary across the corpus: merge commits occur in several repositories, while others follow linear histories. No Git tags or release objects were found in the audited set, but several projects identify states through dated journals, commit references, document versions, numbered decisions, or named iterations. M³GIM contains a substantial deterministic layer for building views, transforming and validating data, reporting quality, auditing data, and checking manual approvals. HerData provides systematic document-level model provenance, while teiCrafter and FemPrompt contain automated and manual test structures that conventional filename-only searches failed to detect. These corrections support a central methodological point: repositories must be interpreted through their own conventions, and negative evidence is inseparable from the procedure by which it was sought.


Several qualitative patterns recur across the projects.

First, the implementations converge strongly on static, frontend-oriented architectures and limited dependency stacks. This partly reflects the technical guidelines of Promptotyping. It may also reflect the tendencies of current coding models and the kinds of projects selected. The cases do not allow these influences to be separated.

Second, the cases with the most detailed domain and process documentation also produced the most finely differentiated project-specific functions. The Notker edition and the ZBZ pipeline contain extensive editorial or stage-specific specifications. CorrespExplorer depends more heavily on a single design document to mediate between generic visualisation conventions and the research perspective. This is an interpretative comparison within one practitioner’s project record, not a measured relationship.

Third, implementation repeatedly produced knowledge that returned to the knowledge base. Interfaces revealed gaps in data models, tests exposed ambiguities in requirements, and generated outputs required more precise statements about uncertainty, verification, or editorial practice. This recurring write-back supports the method’s treatment of documents as revisable project models rather than static instructions.

Fourth, promptotypes appeared at different points in the lifecycle of a project. In M³GIM, interfaces were produced while data capture and modelling were still under way, allowing project participants to discuss an emerging data structure through a concrete artefact. In the Notker case, an Edition Interface was produced before the proposed research project had received funding, making the intended editorial model and technical environment inspectable within the application process. These cases show that Promptotyping can operate prospectively. The artefact may serve as an instrument for defining a project rather than only as an output derived from an already settled one. This increases its value for communication and planning, but it also requires the provisional status of the data, model, and interface to remain explicit.

Finally, the projects do not provide a systematic record of abandonment or failure. The surviving repositories are biased towards work that reached a functional state, and the absence of failed projects cannot be interpreted as demonstrating reliability. Section 5 treats this bias as a methodological limit.

# 5. Scope, Limits, and Priorities for Evaluation

## 5.1 Empirical Scope and Methodological Limits

The method was consolidated primarily through projects led by one hybrid scholar-developer. There is no control condition, no common evaluation protocol across cases, and no systematic record of failed or abandoned attempts; as noted in Section 4.7, the surviving repositories are biased towards work that reached a functional state. Model capabilities also changed during the period in which the projects were developed. Observed improvements cannot therefore be separated cleanly into effects of the method, increased practitioner experience, deeper project attention, or stronger models.

The teaching cases indicate communicability under workshop conditions but do not establish independent long-term use. Documented third-party continuation remains limited.

## 5.2 Conditions of Applicability

Promptotyping cannot externalise knowledge that a practitioner does not possess or cannot recognise as relevant. A well-structured template may support articulation, but it cannot supply missing domain competence. Weak or misleading project documents can guide an agent towards an equally weak or misleading implementation.

The method likewise depends on structured and sufficiently contextualised research data. FAIR and semantic preparation can make relevant structures more explicit, but they do not remove the need to examine how the data was constructed, whose interests it represents, what it excludes, and how its provenance affects interpretation.

## 5.3 Technical, Organisational, Infrastructural, and Social Limits

The technical limits of the static artefact form are stated in Section 4.1 and are not repeated here. Beyond them, agentic organisation creates substantial coordination and auditing work. The author’s experience suggests that monitoring several agents over extended periods becomes cognitively demanding, but this observation has not been measured systematically. The apparent reduction of implementation effort may therefore be accompanied by less visible work in context preparation, permissions, diagnosis, verification, and the reconciliation of divergent outputs. Evaluation must account for who performs this work, which competencies it requires, and whether it is displaced onto researchers or collaborators whose labour is not recognised in conventional accounts of software development.

The documented practice depends on paid access to proprietary frontier systems and suitable agentic coding tools. The paper does not test how far the method remains effective with local, open-weight, or less capable models. Subscription costs, infrastructure concentration, and unequal access reinforce existing differences in technical and scholarly capacity. Researchers and institutions do not control the training data, model weights, update cycles, pricing, or long-term availability of the systems on which present implementations may depend. This dependence sits in tension with the inspectability, durability, and sovereignty sought for research data and software. The paper treats this as a methodological and infrastructural limitation of the documented practice rather than as a measured causal effect.

This dynamic can be described as **asymmetric amplification** (Pollin 2026b). Researchers with well-modelled data, domain expertise, technical literacy, time for verification, and paid access can obtain greater benefits than those without these resources. Training data and model performance also privilege material that is digitised, published, and well represented in dominant languages and formats. Promptotyping inherits these inequalities and may intensify them when the capacity to transform project knowledge into software becomes concentrated among those able to access and govern high-capability systems. The term was introduced in an earlier public essay and is used here as an analytical description of the conditions observed around the method.

The concentration at issue is not only a concentration of model provision. The same proprietary infrastructures increasingly mediate reading, summarisation, modelling, coding, analysis, visualisation, documentation, and assessment. This can narrow the range of readily available representations and workflows, while placing substantial parts of research knowledge work within technical systems whose defaults and limitations are only partly inspectable. The paper does not claim that a complete monopoly of research knowledge work has already occurred. It identifies a tendency towards infrastructural concentration that requires empirical and normative examination. This claim is presented as a bounded interpretation and a priority for future research, not as an empirical result established by the project inventory.

Environmental cost, data governance, confidentiality, copyright, and the treatment of sensitive or restricted research materials also constrain responsible use. Promptotyping does not provide a general ethical licence to submit project data to an external model or service. The permissibility of a workflow depends on the material, legal basis, institutional agreements, model provider, deployment architecture, and applicable research-ethics requirements. Projects working with confidential, personal, culturally sensitive, or contractually restricted data may require synthetic substitutes, local processing, restricted tools, additional agreements, or the exclusion of particular systems. Promptotyping is inappropriate where lawful and ethically acceptable processing cannot be established, where no accountable expert can verify the resulting claims, or where the artefact's status and limitations cannot be communicated adequately.

The maintenance of the knowledge base, monitoring of agents, audit of outputs, correction of errors, and documentation of decisions also constitute substantial work. Promptotyping redistributes labour rather than making it disappear, and this additional maintenance and verification burden may fall unevenly across project roles.

The practical position taken here is one of critical engagement. Current frontier systems enable the work described in this paper, while the need for open, accountable, and sovereign research infrastructure remains unmet. Competence in specifying, steering, and verifying these systems is necessary for responsible present use, but it is not a substitute for reducing infrastructural dependence or for deciding that a given use is inappropriate.

## 5.4 Transferability and Open Questions

The core mechanics of Promptotyping are not obviously limited to the humanities. Versioned documents, structured requirements, agentic implementation, deterministic validation, and expert verification can in principle be applied wherever structured research data must be translated into a project-specific computational form.

The method was nevertheless developed against a specifically humanistic difficulty profile: heterogeneous sources, interpretative modelling, dense domain semantics, uncertain identification, incomplete data, and contested forms of representation. Fields with more regular schemas and more stable representational conventions may face a less demanding version of the problem, but this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated transfer.

The project inventory contains applications in economic and social-work research, yet these cases do not establish general applicability beyond the digital humanities. Testing transfer requires practitioners from other fields and evaluation criteria appropriate to their research practices.

The most important open question is whether a domain expert without a programming background can use the templates and four phases to produce and verify an artefact of scholarly value without continuing hybrid assistance. Possible criteria include the quality of the knowledge base, the ability to identify errors in the generated artefact, the degree of independence after initial support, and the appropriateness of the resulting representation. The teaching cases open this question but do not answer it.

Independently of generative implementation, the knowledge base is designed to provide a structured basis for handover to human developers and Research Software Engineers. Data descriptions, requirements, design decisions, acceptance criteria, and unresolved questions remain useful even where implementation proceeds without an LLM.

## 5.5 Priorities for Evaluation

Future evaluation should distinguish the quality of the knowledge base, the quality of the resulting promptotype and artefact, and the performance of the method as a socio-technical process. Treating these as one outcome would obscure whether a failure originated in the data, the documented project knowledge, agentic implementation, verification, or the organisational conditions under which the work was performed.

**Knowledge-base quality** concerns whether the maintained documents are complete enough for their purpose, internally coherent, current, traceable to sources and decisions, and usable by another contributor or agent instance without undocumented oral explanation. Relevant questions include whether the knowledge base exposes unresolved issues rather than hiding them, whether contradictory statements can be detected and reconciled, and whether a new person can identify the grounds on which a decision was made.

**Promptotype and artefact quality** concerns functional conformance, source and data fidelity, representational adequacy, usability for the intended scholarly activity, verifiability, accessibility, and the clarity of the documented basis of acceptance. An artefact can satisfy tests while representing the research material inadequately; conversely, a plausible representation may remain unusable or technically fragile. Evaluation therefore requires both formal checks and domain judgement.

**Method quality** concerns the distribution of effort and intervention across Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, Implementation, assessment, verification, and write-back. Relevant measures include the number and type of human interventions, recurrent error categories, the time and expertise required to diagnose and correct failures, the extent to which corrections become reusable project knowledge, model and harness dependence, and the ability of an independent researcher or developer to continue the work.

**Transferability** should be assessed through independent use rather than workshop completion alone. A strong test would provide domain researchers with structured data, the method specification, and document templates, then examine whether they can create, inspect, revise, and accept a promptotype without sustained intervention by the originating scholar-developer. Such evaluation should record not only whether an artefact was produced but also the adequacy of its representation, the quality of its knowledge base, the participants’ ability to identify errors, and the conditions under which outside assistance became necessary.

**Comparative evaluation** should test Promptotyping against plausible alternatives, including direct conversational prompting, conventional requirements and development workflows, notebook-based analysis, and other forms of specification-driven agentic development. The purpose is not to establish a universal winner but to identify which project conditions make maintained project knowledge, write-back, and versioned acceptance particularly valuable.

A follow-up study should compare at least three conditions: conventional work without generative assistance, prompt-based work without a maintained project knowledge base, and Promptotyping with the four forms of work and explicit acceptance records. Participants should include domain experts without programming backgrounds, hybrid scholar-developers, and Research Software Engineers. Comparable bounded tasks should be evaluated for knowledge-base completeness and consistency, functional conformance, source fidelity, representational adequacy, error detection, number and type of human interventions, time spent on maintenance and verification, independence after initial support, and quality of handover. Model and harness should be recorded as experimental variables rather than treated as neutral infrastructure. Failed and abandoned attempts must be retained so that the evaluation does not reproduce the survivorship bias of the present project inventory.

## 5.6 Conclusion

Promptotyping addresses the distance between modelled research data and the project-specific digital artefacts through which those data can be investigated, edited, verified, or interpreted. It treats that passage not as a neutral technical conversion but as a sequence of scholarly and engineering decisions.

The method externalises the part of this translation that can be articulated in an evolving project knowledge base. Promptotyping Documents describe the data, record requirements and design decisions, preserve process knowledge, guide agentic implementation, and provide the maintained reference against which resulting artefacts are assessed. Implementation can reveal limitations in the data, assumptions, or specification, and these findings return to the knowledge base.

Each accepted iteration yields an identifiable and versioned promptotype: a coherent state connecting maintained project knowledge, the digital research artefact, the documented basis of acceptance, and the research-data state on which the artefact operates. A promptotype can support exploration, internal use, publication, or handover, and it can become the starting point for a later iteration. Acceptance closes one iteration without claiming that the project has become final.

The knowledge base is not authoritative independently of the people who work with it. Its authority relative to implementation arises through the accountable engagement of researchers and technical contributors who interpret, maintain, contest, and approve its contents. Promptotyping therefore does not transfer scholarly authority to an AI agent. It changes the form in which scholarly knowledge guides implementation and the range of implementation work that can be performed from it.

This amplification is not socially or infrastructurally neutral. Its benefits depend on access to data, expertise, verification capacity, and model infrastructure, while its use may deepen dependence on a small number of proprietary systems. Responsible application therefore requires both project-level judgement about whether the systems may be used with particular materials and broader work towards open and sovereign research infrastructure.

The contribution is one of amplification rather than replacement: agentic work can extend the reach of scholarly and hybrid expertise across implementation steps, sessions, versions, and handovers, while domain modelling, contextual interpretation, technical judgement, and verification by the Critical Expert remain necessary, as Section 3.4 argues.

The documented projects demonstrate the feasibility and breadth of the method within the author’s practice across heterogeneous data types and several interface functions. They do not establish comparative efficacy or independent transferability. As the cross-case observations in Section 4.7 show, promptotypes need not appear only after a project has stabilised; they may participate in the formation of the project itself.

Further work should test Promptotyping with independent researchers, compare it with alternative development practices, establish appropriate criteria for knowledge-base, promptotype, and artefact quality, and examine where domain experts can work without sustained hybrid support. The public method specification and templates provide a basis for that work, while versioned project repositories preserve the documented practice from which the method was consolidated.
