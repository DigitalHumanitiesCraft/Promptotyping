# Promptotyping. Translating Research Data into Research Artefacts through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering

## Abstract

Digital research data become operational for scholarly work through software, yet generic tools do not necessarily support the project-specific distinctions on which that work depends. Developing software adapted to particular research questions, data models, and scholarly practices has traditionally required resources that individual researchers and small projects often lack. **This paper presents *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering.** Its organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base from which LLM-based agents create and revise artefacts and into which findings and corrections from implementation, verification, and validation are written back. Responsibility for the consequential judgements through which each iteration is validated and accepted remains with accountable contributors. The paper consolidates a method developed through recorded experimental practice since 2023, led by a single scholar-developer across Digital Humanities projects and teaching contexts. While the documented cases demonstrate the method’s feasibility and range within this practice, its comparative effectiveness and transferability remain to be evaluated.

## 1. Introduction

Scholarly work with digital research data is necessarily mediated by software. Research activities such as enrichment and analysis may require the operationalisation of selected research concepts through procedures that make their instances identifiable, measurable, or available for downstream processing (Pichler and Reiter 2022). More broadly, enrichment, analysis, and dissemination[^1] rely on representations and procedures through which modelled distinctions become processable, queryable, and presentable by software. A CSV file can be opened as searchable text, interpreted as a sortable table in a spreadsheet, or imported into *Gephi*[^2] as a node or edge table when its records and attributes conform to structures the software can process. Relations encoded in project-specific TEI[^3], by contrast, can be represented as a network only after relevant entities, relations, and uncertainties have been interpreted and mapped to a graph model and a compatible input format. A tool such as *Gephi* can process only those distinctions that have been expressed through graph structures and attributes supported by its representational and operational model. **Project-specific semantics and uncertainty can therefore inform software-supported analysis and visualisation only insofar as they have been encoded in structures that the tool can process.**

Large language models (LLMs)[^4] can reduce parts of the effort required both to map research data to the models and formats expected by existing software and to implement more closely adapted ways of working with those data. Structured or sufficiently semi-structured data can often be transformed deterministically through an explicit mapping. Where the relevant structure is not yet explicit, it must first be recognised or interpreted. If an LLM or another machine-learning system performs this step, the resulting output is probabilistic. Generative models can accordingly be used at different stages of research workflows in which digital research data are created, transformed, enriched, analysed, or made accessible (Pollin et al. 2025). **They may generate structured output, produce code for deterministic transformations, or combine probabilistic and deterministic operations within a workflow designed for the material and task.**[^5]

Mapping data into an existing tool may require less implementation effort than developing a project-specific artefact, but it also confines subsequent work to the distinctions, operations, and forms of interaction that the tool provides. LLM-based code generation can reduce parts of the implementation effort that have traditionally made project-specific development difficult for individual researchers and small projects. **It can therefore make it practicable to develop and compare a wider range of project-specific digital research artefacts, allowing software to be adapted more closely to particular research data, questions, and scholarly practices rather than requiring those practices to conform exclusively to generic tools.**

These possibilities do not eliminate the need for verification and validation. Probabilistic model outputs may be unsupported or internally inconsistent, while deterministic transformations may be incorrectly specified or implemented. Technical verification establishes whether an output or implementation conforms to formalised requirements, such as a schema, test, constraint, or specified mapping. Scholarly validation assesses whether the underlying representation is warranted by the source material and adequate for its intended research purpose.[^6] **An implementation may therefore be technically correct while failing scholarly validation because the requirement or mapping it faithfully realises is itself inadequate.**

This paper introduces *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge. The name combines *prompt* and *prototyping* and retains the established function of a prototype as a provisional implementation through which requirements and design possibilities can be examined and refined. Such artefacts may take different forms, including interfaces, transformations, workflows, and research tools designed to support particular scholarly questions, data models, and practices. **The paper asks how scholars, research software engineers, and AI agents can collaborate through documented project knowledge to iteratively develop project-specific digital research artefacts that help project contributors clarify requirements and examine which forms of scholarly work the project’s research data can support.** Section 1 formulates the problem and establishes the conceptual and technical basis for the method. Section 2 reconstructs the method’s development from documented practice and derives a systematic account of its knowledge architecture, working processes, and forms of verification, validation, and acceptance. It also defines the *promptotype*, the identifiable and versioned state that an accepted iteration yields. Section 3 examines the epistemic and methodological consequences of translating scholarly knowledge into agent-readable specifications and functional artefacts. Section 4 analyses the documented projects and recurrent artefact forms through which the method was developed and consolidated. Section 5 defines the method’s scope and limits and outlines priorities for evaluation and transfer.

### 1.1 Translating Research Data into Digital Research Artefacts through Scholar-Centred Design

*Digital research data* broadly include digitally encoded materials collected, created, transformed, or used in the course of research. The term does not designate a uniform or self-evident class of objects. What counts as research data depends on the disciplinary and project context in which material is selected and interpreted (Geiger 2024). This paper focuses on structured research data: selective representations in which particular aspects of research material are made computationally explicit and thereby available for defined operations. This dependence on selection and interpretation is particularly consequential in the humanities, where historical and cultural sources often resist complete or unambiguous representation. Features of these sources are not transferred into a dataset without interpretation (Drucker 2011; Schöch 2013) but become addressable through scholarly modelling decisions, including decisions about how entities are related and how missing or uncertain information is represented. Although research data may themselves be understood as digital research artefacts in a broad sense, this paper distinguishes the two by their function within the research process. ***Digital research artefacts*** **are project-specific operational forms through which researchers work with structured research data for a defined scholarly purpose.**

Modelling provides a basis for this operationalisation by making selected aspects of a research domain explicit and relating them to a particular scholarly purpose (Ciula et al. 2023). It may involve conceptual accounts of the domain and research context as well as formal data models that specify which distinctions can be represented and processed computationally (Flanders and Jannidis 2019). These distinctions may be expressed through project-specific models, community standards such as TEI, or Semantic Web technologies.[^7] Such formalisation can contribute to FAIR preparation (Wilkinson et al. 2016) by supporting the machine-actionability, interoperability, and reuse of research data across systems.[^8] **It does not, however, determine how researchers should work with those data in a particular project. The same data may support different research questions and therefore require different operational forms.**

Digital tool criticism has accordingly argued that software participates in scholarly knowledge production rather than serving as a neutral means of access (van Es, Wieringa, and Schäfer 2018; Koolen, van Gorp, and van Ossenbruggen 2019; Fickers 2020; Herrmann et al. 2023). Translating research data into a digital research artefact is therefore not merely a conversion between technical formats. **Software shapes which modelled distinctions become visible and what researchers can do with them.** Generic tools provide predefined representations and forms of interaction that may not correspond to a particular project’s data and scholarly practices. Researchers may consequently have to adapt their inquiry to assumptions developed for other purposes. Project-specific digital research artefacts remain selective, but allow those selections to be made and examined in relation to the project’s own data and purpose.

Scholar-Centred Design was developed in my dissertation as a method for translating scholarly perspectives into project-specific requirements for digital research artefacts (Pollin 2025a, 89–98). It combines requirements engineering with sustained engagement with scholars, their source material, and the modelling decisions through which the data were produced. The method emerged from work on the *Bookkeeping Ontology*[^9] and DEPCHA,[^10] which used a shared model of historical accounting transactions to normalise and publish heterogeneous datasets (Pollin 2019). Two contrasting use cases showed why this interoperable basis was not sufficient. Work on the *Wheaton Day Book* required ways of tracing social relationships and forms of exchange through economic transactions, whereas work on the *Jahrrechnungen der Stadt Basel* required aggregation and comparison across administrative and temporal hierarchies. **The shared representation of transactions supported both forms of inquiry but did not determine which distinctions should be foregrounded or which operations and interface structures were required for each investigation.**

These requirements could be specified, but the project lacked the development capacity to realise them as functional, project-specific artefacts. Translating scholarly requirements into software has commonly involved collaboration between domain specialists and technical contributors, including Research Software Engineers (Edmond 2005; Baxter et al. 2012; Cohen et al. 2021; Kemman 2021). Such collaboration remains essential for artefacts that require dependable long-term operation, institutional integration, security, or supported use by third parties. The necessary expertise and resources, however, are not equally available to individual researchers and small projects (Cohen et al. 2021; Carver et al. 2022).

LLM-based systems can reduce parts of the implementation work, making it more practicable to develop project-specific digital research artefacts within the research process. Not all relevant requirements can be determined in advance. Provisional artefacts make proposed distinctions and possible ways of working with the data available for examination. They can reveal problems in the specification, show what the data can and cannot support, and help researchers compare alternative approaches. **Implementation thereby becomes part of determining both how researchers wish to work with their data and what those data can support.** Promptotyping organises this iterative process through maintained project knowledge that guides human contributors and LLM-based AI agents as they develop and revise artefacts together, and into which findings from implementation are incorporated as revised project knowledge.

### 1.2 Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI Harnesses

LLMs have become increasingly capable of generating code and carrying out extended software-development tasks. Their effective and responsible use, where such use is warranted, requires more than selecting a capable model or formulating an effective prompt.[^11] Results also depend on the information available to the system, the organisation of the work performed by LLM-based AI agents, and the technical environment through which they interact with a project. These interdependent informational, organisational, and technical concerns are addressed here through Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI harnesses.

*Context Engineering* refers here to the systematic maintenance and provision of the information an LLM-based system requires for its work. It extends *Prompt Engineering*, understood as the iterative development of a prompt through changes to its content or prompting technique (Schulhoff et al. 2024), from individual prompts to the wider informational environment in which they are interpreted (Mei et al. 2025). Within Promptotyping, this environment records the project’s current understanding of its data and purpose, together with the decisions and unresolved questions relevant to implementation. Context Engineering does not consist in placing all available material into a context window. It maintains an inspectable body of project knowledge from which task-specific context can be selected and refreshed.

An *AI agent* is understood here as an LLM-based system that pursues a goal through a sequence of tool-supported actions and adapts to intermediate results (Sapkota, Roumeliotis, and Karkee 2026; Wang et al. 2024). Rather than responding only to an isolated input, an agent may inspect project resources, act on them, evaluate the results, and continue its work across several steps. *Agentic Engineering* refers here to the systematic organisation of this extended work. It concerns how tasks are decomposed and coordinated, how agents use tools and respond to intermediate results, when human intervention is required, and how their work is inspected and continued. The term is used broadly because Promptotyping agents work not only on code but also on the documents through which a project is described, directed, and assessed.

An *AI harness* is the technical environment through which an agent receives context, accesses project resources, uses tools, and obtains feedback. It mediates the relation between the model and the environment in which the work is performed. **Agentic capability therefore arises from the combined model–harness–environment system rather than from the model alone** (Zhong and Zhu 2026). A harness may provide controlled access to project files, execution environments, tests, and running artefacts while maintaining state and permissions. It thereby supplies the technical conditions under which Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering can be applied.

Promptotyping is not tied to a particular AI harness or level of delegated agentic work. Its earliest cases were conducted through iterative sequences of prompts, with human direction renewed at each step. Contemporary agentic development systems combine a high-capability LLM with an AI harness that gives the agent controlled access to a persistent project environment.[^12] When this environment is maintained as a Git repository, project documents, code, configuration, tests, and references to relevant research data can be kept together under version control. The unit of agentic work is therefore no longer an isolated prompt or code fragment, but the versioned project environment as a whole. Beyond increased model capability, the methodologically relevant change is the growing amount and variety of work that can be delegated within a persistent, tool-accessible project context between successive human interventions.

As AI agents become capable of completing increasingly extended, multi-step computational tasks between human interventions, the knowledge required to guide their work can no longer remain confined to an individual prompt, a transient conversation, or continuous human direction.[^13] **It must persist beyond individual interactions while remaining available to project contributors for inspection and correction.**[^14]

## 2. Promptotyping as a Method

Promptotyping developed through LLM-supported experiments in research and teaching conducted since 2023. Early work externalised and distilled project knowledge into task-specific representations that could guide implementation beyond individual prompts.[^15] As repository-based coding agents became able to work directly with project documents, research data, code, and running artefacts, these representations developed into an evolving and versioned project knowledge base from which implementation could proceed. The term *Promptotyping* was first used in my dissertation and subsequently developed in a blog post and a contribution to a scholarly portal (Pollin 2025a; Pollin 2025b; Pollin 2026a). The present paper consolidates the method, while its specification, document templates, and revision history are maintained in the public [Promptotyping repository](https://github.com/DigitalHumanitiesCraft/Promptotyping).

### 2.1 Promptotyping and the Project Knowledge Base

***Promptotyping*** **is an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering.** Its organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base represented in interrelated documents. Researchers and contributors maintain that knowledge and direct the implementation and revision tasks assigned to LLM-based agents. The artefact is developed through the continuing interaction between maintained project knowledge, agentic execution, and human interpretation and judgement.

The method is knowledge-driven because the research data and the project knowledge base fulfil distinct functions. The data remain the project’s source basis and are processed through explicit, inspectable operations. The knowledge base records the project’s current understanding of those data, its scholarly purpose, and the decisions governing implementation. It is a knowledge base in the sense of an explicitly represented body of knowledge on which an agent operates (Russell and Norvig 2020), although it takes a semi-formal documentary form and supports no formal inference. It neither replaces the research data nor reproduces them in full, but provides a task-relevant account of their meaning and of the conditions under which they can be used.

The project knowledge base is maintained through interrelated *knowledge documents*. These are bounded representations distilled from fuller research material and organised so that human contributors can inspect and revise them while agents can use them as context for assigned work. Their filenames and distribution are not constitutive of the method. A small project may combine several functions in one document, whereas a larger project may distribute them across a more differentiated documentary structure.

*Declarative knowledge documents* state what the project currently takes to be the case or requires the artefact to realise. A `data.md` may describe the structure, semantics, provenance, gaps, and uncertainties of the research data. A `requirements.md` connects scholarly practices with expected system behaviour. A `design.md` records how modelled distinctions should become visible or actionable, including how the artefact should represent uncertainty, missingness, and provenance rather than leaving such decisions to model or software defaults.

*Process knowledge documents* preserve how the project’s understanding developed and why particular decisions were taken. A `journal.md` records attempted approaches, failures, decisions, and unresolved questions, making the work resumable and open to subsequent criticism. More consequential decisions may be preserved in dedicated decision records. Version control can show what changed, while process knowledge documents explain the reasoning that made the change meaningful.

*Action knowledge documents* translate maintained project knowledge into instructions for agentic work. A file such as `CLAUDE.md` or `AGENTS.md` may define the technical baseline, working procedures, permissions, and checks that govern an agent’s behaviour. Separating these comparatively volatile instructions from more stable descriptions of the data and scholarly purpose prevents a temporary implementation directive from silently altering the project’s maintained account of what its material means.

These categories identify the primary function of a document rather than enforcing a one-to-one classification of files. A validation record, for example, may combine a declarative verdict with a process account of the material examined and the procedure used. The distinction nevertheless has a diagnostic function. A factually incorrect output directs attention towards the declarative knowledge on which it rests. A violation of the intended workflow may indicate an inadequate action document. Where the rationale for a decision cannot be recovered, the process layer has failed to preserve relevant knowledge. **The typology therefore helps direct corrections towards the kind of project knowledge that proved inadequate rather than accumulating local patches in the implementation.**

Knowledge documents may be supplemented by *derived context artefacts*. These are reports produced from project resources through an identified and repeatable process. A script may, for example, generate a current profile of an XML corpus, report broken references, or render the results of schema validation. Such artefacts can be refreshed when the underlying data change and can expose structures that would otherwise require repeated inspection of the complete corpus. They report observations derived from a specified data state; they do not acquire the authority of maintained scholarly statements about what those observations mean. Their source and generating process must therefore remain explicit.

Knowledge documents are operative rather than merely retrospective. Human contributors use them to inspect assumptions, coordinate decisions, and assess results. Agents use selected documents when planning and carrying out assigned work. Their persistence allows project knowledge to remain available when a conversation ends, an agent instance changes, or another contributor continues the implementation.

Context Engineering establishes the knowledge available for the task; Agentic Engineering organises how implementation proceeds from it. The two remain interdependent. A carefully maintained knowledge base does not determine how an agent should proceed, while a well-organised agentic workflow cannot compensate for an inadequate account of the data or research purpose.

The project knowledge base is not tied to a particular document environment. It may be maintained as a compact set of versioned files, an interlinked note vault or wiki, or another inspectable and revisable documentary system.[^16] The organisation of persistent project knowledge must also be distinguished from the mechanism through which task-specific material enters an agent’s working context. Documents may be supplied directly, selected through repository search, or combined with retrieval-augmented generation where large collections cannot usefully be distilled into maintained summaries (Lewis et al. 2020). These approaches can be combined according to the material and task. Knowledge documents state the project’s maintained understanding and decisions. Retrieval can supply source passages, records, or research literature pertinent to a particular task. Derived context artefacts can provide current structural observations. Direct file access can allow an agent to inspect selected project resources. These mechanisms supplement rather than replace the maintained account of the project’s purpose, data semantics, and criteria of assessment.

Nominal context capacity does not imply that all information placed in a context window will be used uniformly or reliably (Hong, Troynikov, and Huber 2025). Promptotyping therefore treats selection as a necessary part of context design rather than treating accumulation as a substitute for it. In a common arrangement, an agent receives a distilled account of the research data, while explicit code, queries, or transformations process the complete data outside the model context.[^17] The description does not replace the data, and direct access to the data does not replace the knowledge required to interpret them.

The project knowledge base is not prepared once and then executed. It is established, tested, and revised through four recurrent forms of work, which organise the development of a promptotype.

### 2.2 Forms of Work and Iteration

Promptotyping is organised around *Preparation*, *Exploration*, *Distillation*, and *Implementation*. **These are recurrent forms of work rather than fixed project stages.** Each has a distinct object and a provisional completion condition, but their depth, sequence, and recurrence depend on the state of the project. Implementation may expose shortcomings in the specification, the understanding of the data, or the source basis itself. Such findings can return the work to any earlier form, although the most frequent return is to Distillation, where maintained project knowledge is revised.

The Critical Expert specifies and revises the knowledge documents and validates the resulting artefact. Purpose-specific acceptance connects maintained project knowledge, the resulting digital research artefact, the referenced research-data state, and the documented grounds of acceptance as an identifiable promptotype.

*Preparation* establishes the source basis, research context, and current purpose from which the artefact is to be developed. It brings the research data, their documentation, applicable standards, existing models, and relevant scholarly knowledge into an accessible project environment. Requirements Engineering belongs to Preparation because a digital research artefact cannot be specified independently of the scholarly activities it is intended to support. Requirements may derive from the researcher’s domain knowledge or from collaborative Scholar-Centred Design, while user stories and acceptance criteria connect descriptions of scholarly practice with expected system behaviour (Lucassen et al. 2016). Preparation does not require the research question, data model, or intended artefact to be settled permanently. It requires the current basis to be explicit enough that later changes can be identified as revisions rather than absorbed invisibly into implementation. An undefined category, unexplained absence, or uncertain date may otherwise acquire an arbitrary operational meaning. Preparation is provisionally sufficient when the source basis is accessible and the current purpose and assumptions are explicit enough to support systematic examination.

*Exploration* examines the relation between the intended scholarly activity and what the available research data can support. Establishing what the data cannot support, and what further modelling or contextualisation would be required, is as important as identifying what can be implemented. Exploration combines scholarly inspection with deterministic profiling, experiments, and model-assisted proposals. Generated mappings and representations are treated as hypotheses about the relation between data and inquiry rather than as findings that can be accepted without domain judgement. Its depth depends on the project. Familiar and well-understood data may require little more than the externalisation of existing knowledge, whereas unfamiliar or heterogeneous material may require substantial profiling and comparison of alternative operationalisations. Exploration is provisionally sufficient when feasibility, limitations, defensible alternatives, and unresolved questions have been documented well enough to guide subsequent decisions.

*Distillation* translates the understanding produced through Preparation and Exploration into the knowledge documents from which implementation proceeds. It is the principal documentary operation of Context Engineering within the method. Distillation selects and organises the project knowledge required for the current purpose while leaving the fuller sources and research data available for direct processing and inspection. It is not reducible to summarisation or token minimisation. The intellectual work lies in determining which distinctions, constraints, uncertainties, examples, and exceptions must remain available for implementation and checking. Distillation can therefore be understood as pragmatic modelling. It creates a selective representation for particular human and agentic addressees at a particular point in the project, and the same material may need to be distilled differently when the research purpose, intended artefact, or working environment changes.

The sufficiency of Distillation is practical rather than formal. A new agent instance, supplied with the relevant knowledge documents and access to the project resources, should be able to understand the project’s current logic and continue the assigned work without relying on an undocumented oral explanation. This test cannot establish that the knowledge base is complete, but it can expose omissions when the agent cannot proceed, requires unavailable information, or produces an implausible result. A description too vague to implement is often also too vague to function as adequate documentation. Conversely, an instruction may be executable while remaining inadequate in scholarly terms if its assumptions cannot be justified.

*Implementation* makes maintained project knowledge actionable through an LLM-based agent operating within an AI harness. The agent carries out bounded implementation and revision tasks, while the harness mediates its access to project files, execution environments, tools, tests, and the running artefact. Implementation proceeds through inspectable increments rather than a single act of generation. An early increment may prioritise making an artefact visible and runnable, but it remains provisional until its consequences have been examined. Bounded increments allow contributors to assess the direction of development before further assumptions become embedded in a larger implementation.

Implementation is not merely the execution of a settled specification. A running transformation, interface, or capture environment may expose a missing distinction, unsupported assumption, or conflict between the research model and its operational use. The appropriate return depends on what has been learned. An inadequate instruction normally requires revision of the knowledge documents, a changed understanding of what the data can support returns the work to Exploration, and newly available material or altered project conditions may require renewed Preparation. These returns are not exceptional procedures but expressions of the iterative relation among the four forms of work.

A correction becomes methodologically consequential when it is incorporated into maintained project knowledge. Changing generated code without revising an inadequate requirement or design decision may repair one artefact state while leaving the same failure available to recur. **Write-back allows subsequent implementation to proceed from the revised understanding and is therefore the mechanism through which implementation findings become durable project knowledge rather than an additional phase of the method.**

A *Promptotyping iteration* may contain several such cycles. It concludes when the maintained project knowledge, the resulting digital research artefact, the referenced research-data state, and the documented grounds of acceptance form a coherent and identifiable state for a stated purpose. **This accepted iteration state is a *promptotype*.** Not every artefact generated with an LLM meets this threshold. A provisional artefact remains a precursor when its relation to project knowledge, data, and acceptance cannot be reconstructed.

The promptotype relation allows researchers, technical contributors, and other stakeholders to examine the same operational state rather than negotiating exclusively through abstract descriptions. The *Critical Expert* represented in Figure 1 may be one person or a group with distributed responsibilities. The role denotes the competence and responsibility required to determine whether the project knowledge represents the research material adequately and whether the artefact warrants acceptance for its intended purpose. **An agent may propose mappings, designs, or assessments, but producing a plausible proposal and accepting responsibility for its adequacy remain distinct acts.** How these components operate together, and what each form of checking contributes to an accepted promptotype, becomes concrete in the Stefan Zweig HTR pipeline.

### 2.3 From Project Knowledge to an Accepted Promptotype

The Stefan Zweig Digital (SZD) HTR pipeline provides a worked example of how maintained project knowledge is translated into a digital research artefact and examined before acceptance. The pipeline processes an evolving collection of facsimile scans from the Stefan Zweig estate at the Literaturarchiv Salzburg. TEI metadata describes the heterogeneous material and provides object-specific context for producing diplomatic transcriptions intended to make the estate readable and searchable without normalising its textual features.[^18] The example illustrates how the components of Promptotyping operate together; the project’s development history and evidential status are examined separately in Section 4.2.

Preparation brought the facsimiles, TEI metadata, source typology, transcription purpose, and existing editorial knowledge into a common project environment. The metadata was not treated only as catalogue information. Differences among document types and hands affected which instructions and processing routes were appropriate. Structured descriptions of the material therefore became part of the context through which the pipeline organised transcription.

Exploration examined how vision-language-model transcription behaved across the heterogeneous material and which distinctions the workflow had to preserve. The relevant question was not only which model produced the most plausible text. Exploration also examined where object-specific instructions were required, which textual features could be represented reliably, and where authoritative checking had to enter the workflow. Generated transcriptions and proposed processing routes were treated as material for comparison rather than as accepted results.

Distillation incorporated the resulting understanding into the project knowledge base. Declarative knowledge documents describe the source material, transcription purpose, and annotation conventions. Uncertain or illegible readings, deletions, insertions, and other textual features are represented through defined markup rather than silently regularised. Process knowledge documents preserve the decisions through which the workflow developed, while action knowledge documents translate accepted editorial and technical decisions into instructions governing how the agents process the material and how generated outputs are to be marked and examined.

Derived context artefacts supplement this maintained knowledge. Corpus profiles and schema-validation reports can be regenerated from the current project state. They remain distinguishable from the maintained scholarly account of what the observations they report mean and how they should affect transcription or checking.

Implementation makes this combined knowledge actionable. For a particular task, the AI harness assembles a working context from the maintained knowledge documents and any additional material needed for that task. The agent then carries out bounded work such as implementing a transformation, modifying a processing route, creating an interface component, or adding a formal check. The complete facsimile and metadata collections need not be placed in the model context. The resulting operations can act on project resources through the execution environment supplied by the harness. Figure 2 represents this arrangement by separating persistent project knowledge from the task-specific working context and by distinguishing implementation from the authority required for validation and acceptance.

![Project knowledge, working context, implementation, and authority in Promptotyping](assets/figures/svg/figure-02-knowledge-context-authority.svg)

**Figure 2. Project knowledge, working context, implementation, and authority in Promptotyping.** Task-specific working context is assembled from the versioned project knowledge base and, where required, from retrieved source material or derived context artefacts. An AI agent operating within a harness carries out bounded implementation and revision tasks that produce or modify a digital research artefact. Deterministic verification and agentic review provide evidence about the artefact but do not authorise scholarly claims. Critical Expert validation assesses source fidelity, design, and interpretation, and only responsible human contributors accept an iteration for its stated purpose.

The project knowledge base constrains implementation without determining it completely. Natural-language descriptions retain ambiguity, and different agent runs may realise the same requirement differently. A detailed specification therefore does not guarantee an adequate result. The knowledge base provides the maintained reference from which implementation proceeds and against which its consequences can be examined.

This arrangement is related to Spec-Driven Development, in which persistent specifications provide a governing reference for agentic software development (Macedo 2026). Promptotyping differs in the content and authority of those specifications. Its knowledge documents describe not only intended system behaviour but also research data and sources that precede the artefact and carry their own semantics, uncertainty, and scholarly history. The researcher is therefore not merely a stakeholder requesting functionality but remains responsible for claims about the material being operationalised.

This also distinguishes Promptotyping from Vibe Coding, understood as generating code from natural-language instructions while accepting the implementation without thorough review (Sarkar and Drosos 2025). Promptotyping may use rapid generation during Exploration, but a runnable interface is not accepted merely because it appears plausible or functions technically. Maintained project knowledge, inspectable implementation, and differentiated checking are constitutive of the method.

Implementation in the SZD pipeline produces several kinds of feedback. Formal checks can establish whether a file parses, conforms to a schema, or satisfies another explicitly represented constraint. The running artefact provides visual and interactive feedback that may expose problems not captured by formal tests. An agent may also compare an output with a source or stated criterion and report apparent discrepancies. **These procedures provide different forms of evidence and must not be treated as interchangeable.**

*Deterministic verification* applies where a formal rule decides whether a specified condition has been met. Schemas, tests, constraints, and build procedures can be executed without domain judgement (IEEE 2025). An agent may run these checks and act on unambiguous failures within its assigned scope. Passing verification establishes conformity to the formalised condition, not the scholarly adequacy of that condition.

*Agentic review* uses an LLM-based system to compare an output with a source, requirement, or other reference and to report apparent discrepancies, an arrangement discussed as LLM-as-a-judge. Recent evaluations show that such judges agree with domain experts only partially and perform most reliably where a human-set reference is available (Krumdick et al. 2025; Szymanski et al. 2025). Such review can extend checking across material that a person could not examine at the same frequency or scale. It remains a generative assessment and does not authorise the output it reviews. Its report is evidence for subsequent judgement rather than a validation verdict.

*Critical Expert validation* applies where assessment depends on source knowledge, interpretation, or design judgement, and is exercised in the role defined in Section 2.2. This authority is not transferred to the agent.

*Acceptance testing* occupies a related but narrower position. Acceptance criteria may allow an agent or deterministic procedure to test whether a stated interaction or output has been implemented. Such testing can establish conformance to the requirement as written. It cannot establish that the requirement itself adequately represents the scholarly practice from which it was derived. Despite the shared term, it also remains distinct from the acceptance of an iteration described below; passing every stated criterion does not itself constitute acceptance. The adequacy of the criteria becomes visible chiefly in observed use, for which usability evaluation provides established procedures such as thinking aloud, and which no single form of checking replaces (Hertzum 2024; Benito-Santos et al. 2026).

| Source fidelity requiring judgement | Critical Expert validation | Comparison with the research material | Assist through review, but not issue the final verdict |
| Conformance to a stated requirement | Acceptance testing | Acceptance criteria in the requirements document | Operate the artefact and report whether the criterion is met |
| Design conformance | Critical Expert validation | Maintained design decisions and examination of the artefact | Produce evidence or alternatives for human assessment |
| Interpretation and contextualisation | Critical Expert validation | Sources, project knowledge, and scholarly judgement | Propose or compare interpretations without authorising them |

The SZD interface preserves the distinction among these forms of checking in the status attached to each transcription. A *human-checked* transcription has been examined page by page against its facsimile. An *agent-checked* transcription indicates that a vision-language model compared the image and transcription, but it does not carry human authorisation. Material that has undergone neither form of checking remains explicitly marked as *unchecked*. These states are not combined into a generic confidence score because they derive from different procedures and carry different authority.

Human corrections also preserve the machine-generated transcription in an edit history. This makes the intervention traceable and prevents the accepted state from appearing as if it had been produced without correction. The distinction is methodological as well as administrative: provenance concerning how an output was generated and checked affects what can be claimed on its basis.

The boundary between verification and validation may change as the project develops. Where Critical Expert validation establishes a recurrent rule that can be formalised, part of the subsequent checking can be converted into deterministic verification. An accepted encoding decision may, for example, become a schema constraint, as test-driven development converts a prior judgement about correct behaviour into an executable check (Fakhoury et al. 2024). The conversion does not operate in the opposite direction. Passing any number of formal checks cannot establish the adequacy of distinctions that were never formalised. Nor can a check that an agent writes for itself substitute for the conversion, since it codifies no judgement external to the generation it tests (Hora and Robbes 2026).

The term *Critical Expert in the Loop* specifies what generic human-in-the-loop arrangements leave open, since effective oversight depends on conditions that mere presence does not supply (Sterz et al. 2024). The required competence includes knowledge of the research material and sufficient understanding of the technical and generative processes through which the artefact was produced. LLM-specific failure modes make this combination consequential. A model may reproduce an assumption embedded in a request rather than challenge it, or fill a gap with a plausible continuation unsupported by the available material (Sharma et al. 2024). Apparently similar tasks may also differ considerably in reliability, a jaggedness examined in Section 3 whose boundary the Critical Expert cannot infer from surface difficulty alone (Dell’Acqua et al. 2023).

The Critical Expert’s responsibility is not limited to detecting errors in generated content. It includes examining the framing that governed what was generated. Alternatives may not have been explored, established conventions may have been reproduced without justification, and absences may be concealed by an apparently coherent interface. Validation must therefore consider both the realised artefact and the possibility space from which it emerged.

Where scholarly and technical responsibilities are distributed, a domain specialist may determine whether an encoding or representation is warranted, while a technical contributor assesses the implementation or organisation of the workflow. The project knowledge base provides a shared and revisable object through which these judgements can be articulated.

Promptotyping can also employ several differentiated agents, although the documented practice is predominantly based on a single agentic working context. Additional agents may be assigned to separate components or checks when the division of labour is explicit and inspectable. Their permissions should follow the principle of least privilege, so that each receives only the project resources required for its assigned work (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975). Increasing the number of agents does not reduce the need for human judgement and may increase the effort required to coordinate and audit their work.

An iteration is accepted when the responsible contributors judge that its maintained project knowledge, the resulting digital research artefact, the referenced research-data state, and the documented grounds of acceptance, including the checking evidence they record, form a coherent state for a stated purpose. Acceptance is therefore distinct from both verification and validation. **Verification establishes conformity to formal rules; validation assesses claims that require judgement; acceptance determines whether the assembled state is adequate for its intended use.**

Acceptance is purpose-specific. A promptotype may support different points in a project lifecycle, from an exploratory demonstrator or internal workflow to a handover package or published artefact. Each purpose requires different acceptance criteria. Closing an iteration does not imply that the project, research data, or artefact has become final. New sources, corrected assumptions, or improved checks may initiate another iteration and produce a later promptotype.

The accepted state must be identifiable. Promptotyping is not tied to GitHub or Semantic Versioning, but the relation on which acceptance rests must be recoverable. A repository release, archived deposit, or another durable snapshot can provide this reference. A re-run with another model should be treated as a new iteration rather than silently identified with the earlier promptotype.

In the SZD workflow, the accepted promptotype therefore connects the maintained account of the source material and transcription purpose with the implemented pipeline and interface, the referenced corpus state, and the documented grounds of acceptance, which record the authority attached to its checked and unchecked outputs.

How this organisation relates to the wider requirements of accountable research software development, and where its limits lie, is the subject of the following section.

### 2.4 Documentation, Accountability, and the Limits of AI-Assisted Development

Research software shapes which computational questions and methods can practically be pursued. Access to software-engineering expertise, training, and support nevertheless remains uneven across the research software lifecycle.[^19] AI-assisted development may lower parts of this implementation barrier by making some computational experiments and research workflows practicable within limited resources. The same accessibility also lowers the threshold for producing technically functional but insufficiently examined artefacts. The informal term *AI slop* is used here for this wider problem.[^20] In research software, it includes interfaces, transformations, and data representations whose assumptions, relation to the research material, or grounds of acceptance cannot be reconstructed.

These risks fall within established concerns of research integrity. The use of generative systems does not reduce researchers’ responsibility for the methods, representations, and claims produced through their work. Guidance on generative AI therefore connects its use to continuing requirements of careful conduct, appropriate disclosure, transparency, and human responsibility.[^21] Whether a generative system should be used remains a prior scholarly, ethical, legal, and institutional decision. Promptotyping addresses the narrower methodological question of how AI-assisted implementation can be organised so that the knowledge guiding it, the evidence produced through examination, and the judgements through which its results are accepted remain open to inspection and correction. That knowledge cannot remain confined to the unstated understanding of one contributor.

The forms of checking defined in Section 2.3 produce evidence with different scope. **Accountability therefore requires a distinction between evidence and authority.** A system’s ability to generate a finding, execute a check, or modify a workflow state does not give it the competence or authority to issue the scholarly or technical judgement that the state may appear to express.

Human involvement alone does not resolve this problem. Consequential judgements must be attributable to contributors with the relevant competence and recognised responsibility, and they must refer to an identifiable artefact state, data state, evidential basis, and purpose. Transparent documentation does not make an agent trustworthy, passing tests does not establish scholarly adequacy, and the presence of a person in the workflow does not guarantee competent validation (Green 2022).[^22] Promptotyping therefore keeps machine-generated findings, administrative workflow states, Critical Expert validation, and acceptance distinguishable even where they are operationally connected. **An agent must never record approval, validation, or acceptance that a responsible person has not explicitly granted.**

This distinction also limits the claims attached to an accepted promptotype. Acceptance concerns the documented relation defined in Section 2.2, held together for a stated purpose. It does not constitute a general endorsement of the model, development process, or software beyond that bounded state. Writing consequential findings back into maintained project knowledge can preserve the reasons for revisions and support later inspection, continuation, and handover. It cannot reproduce every generative interaction. Persistent project knowledge may reduce some forms of provider dependence, but it does not guarantee the portability or exact reproducibility of the development process.[^23]

The scope of Promptotyping remains correspondingly limited. Maintained documentation may be incomplete or mistaken, and its adequacy continues to depend on relevant scholarly and technical expertise. The method cannot determine the legal, ethical, contractual, and institutional conditions under which external generative systems may be used. Nor does it provide the maintenance, security, operational reliability, resourcing, and continued stewardship required for sustainable research software.[^24] Artefacts intended for dependable long-term operation, institutional integration, or supported third-party use therefore remain within the domain of professional Research Software Engineering.

**Promptotyping should consequently be understood as a method for organising a bounded form of AI-assisted research software development, not as a comprehensive framework for responsible AI or sustainable research software.** It preserves project knowledge beyond individual interactions, differentiates the evidential scope of checking procedures, keeps consequential judgements attributable to competent contributors, and connects acceptance to an identifiable project state. These provisions do not eliminate the risks of generative implementation, but they provide an inspectable structure through which technically plausible output can be subjected to scholarly and technical judgement.

Translating maintained project knowledge into functional research artefacts also has epistemic consequences that reach beyond the organisation of work, and they are the subject of the next chapter.

## 3. Epistemic and Methodological Implications

What does it mean for computer-based research when generative systems can increasingly perform extended tasks involving code, structured data, formal reasoning, and tool-supported action? Evaluations of frontier systems indicate substantial but uneven advances in these digitally executable capabilities. The duration of predominantly software-oriented tasks that agents can complete with a given probability has increased rapidly, while results in programming, mathematical reasoning, and formal proof demonstrate capabilities that remain highly variable across tasks, benchmarks, and conditions.[^25] This combination of expanding capacity and persistent *jaggedness* provides one basis for the concept of *asymmetric amplification*. Generative systems do not automate research as a whole or support every research activity equally. They can act most directly where relevant materials and operations are digitally represented, while the resulting leverage depends on the task, the available project knowledge, the capacity to assess results, and access to suitable models and infrastructure.[^26]

Coding is a central mediating capability because it allows articulated requirements and domain representations to be translated into executable transformations, interfaces, analyses, and workflows. Promptotyping directs this implementation capacity towards project-specific digital research artefacts through which researchers can work with their own data, models, purposes, and practices. Its epistemic significance therefore lies not simply in producing software more efficiently, but in widening the range of computational forms through which researchers can investigate their material, compare operationalisations, and develop their questions, models, and interpretations.

### 3.1 Research Artefacts and the Amplification of Computer-Based Research

Digital Humanities scholarship has long treated practices such as building, interface design, and visualisation not only as means of presenting findings but also as forms of scholarly investigation. Prototypes can function as experiments and arguments because their structures and interactions embody claims about the material they organise, while information visualisation and exploratory interfaces can support movement from overview and browsing towards comparison, interpretation, and more focused enquiry.[^27] A project-specific digital research artefact gives functional form to a selective scholarly understanding by determining which entities, relations, uncertainties, comparisons, and actions become available. This selectivity remains whether the artefact is implemented manually or through an LLM-based agent, since generated interfaces, transformations, and workflows may reproduce conventions and assumptions that were never deliberately selected or justified.

The cases examined in this paper illustrate three related forms of operationalisation. In the Notker project, a functional edition interface made an editorial and organisational model of textual layers, language changes, sigla, and project scope available for inspection and coordination. In M³GIM, an interface for displaying, filtering, and entering structured archival information allowed project partners to examine the practical consequences of the data model and to identify distinctions requiring revision. In the Stefan Zweig Digital HTR pipeline (SZD-HTR), Promptotyping operationalised a research workflow connecting heterogeneous sources, metadata-guided transcription, formal checking, and human validation. These artefacts respectively support the examination of a scholarly model, collaborative reflection on research data, and the development of a project-specific workflow, but each makes an account of research material and practice available in a functional form through which its consequences can be experienced and assessed.

Such interaction may produce the *backtalk* described in reflective-design literature, in which implemented representations disclose implications that were not fully anticipated (Schön 1996; Goldschmidt 2003). **The research contribution lies not in the existence of a runnable artefact alone, but in the interpretation and revision enabled by its construction and use.** Promptotyping can amplify computer-based research by allowing scholarly assumptions to become actionable, examinable, and revisable within the research process.

### 3.2 Research Data and Project Knowledge as Mediating Structures

Project-specific research artefacts depend on research data that are already selective, machine-actionable representations (Owens 2011; Posner 2015). Schemas, controlled vocabularies, ontologies, and other formal data models can make selected structures, constraints, provenance, and uncertainty computationally addressable, but they do not contain the complete research purpose, determine how researchers should work with the data, or establish the scholarly adequacy of what has been formalised.

Maintained project knowledge mediates between this formal and empirical basis and a particular functional artefact by relating the data to current assumptions, requirements, examples, limitations, and design decisions. This mediation requires selection because neither the complete research data nor the full range of relevant scholarly knowledge can simply be equated with an agent’s task-specific working context.

Promptotyping Documents therefore function as semi-formal conceptual models that abbreviate the project for a particular purpose while remaining inspectable and revisable by responsible contributors (Stachowiak 1973; Mayr and Thalheim 2021). Their semi-formality distinguishes them from both transient prompting and fully formal specification. It provides enough structure to guide implementation while preserving points at which interpretation remains necessary and conformity to a document cannot by itself establish scholarly adequacy.

Their epistemic importance lies in maintaining an inspectable relation between scholarly understanding and functional implementation, so that findings arising from an artefact can be attributed to the code, requirements, design, data assumptions, or underlying model and written back into a revised account of the project rather than remaining local corrections confined to a transient interaction.

### 3.3 Amplification, Competence, and the Limits of Externalisation

Promptotyping amplifies the reach of scholarly and technical competence by allowing articulated descriptions of data, requirements, decisions, examples, and procedures to guide more implementation work, persist across sessions and contributors, and inform several artefacts or alternative versions. This amplification remains asymmetric in two respects. It varies across research activities, since current systems intervene most directly where the relevant materials and operations are also accessible through tools. It varies across researchers and projects because its practical value depends on the conditions named at the opening of this chapter together with the ability to articulate relevant distinctions and the technical support available. The jaggedness observed in model performance reinforces rather than weakens this argument. Benchmark capability does not translate uniformly into productivity within mature projects or demanding research settings.[^28] **What can be externalised is therefore the articulable part of competence rather than competence itself.** Project documents can preserve assumptions, requirements, examples, decisions, rules, and rationales, but they cannot fully contain familiarity with a corpus, disciplinary judgement, recognition of exceptional cases, technical diagnosis, or responsibility for scholarly and technical adequacy. These capacities may be combined in a hybrid scholar-developer or distributed among domain specialists, technical contributors, designers, and Research Software Engineers, with the knowledge base providing a shared object through which their judgements can be coordinated without erasing their differences. Since Promptotyping was developed predominantly through the practice of a hybrid scholar-developer, the documented cases demonstrate most directly how existing scholarly and technical competence can be extended; the limits of that evidence are set out in Section 5.1. Critical Expert validation remains necessary because the consequences disclosed by an artefact become scholarly findings only through competent interpretation, contextualisation, and judgement.

### 3.4 Acceptance, Reconstructability, and the Status of the Promptotype

An accepted promptotype is a bounded and identifiable scholarly state, stabilised for purposes that range from internal use to publication. Publication introduces additional obligations because external users must be able to understand the purpose, data basis, limitations, and status of the released artefact. Since generative implementation is non-deterministic and model-dependent, running the same project documents again may produce different code or another artefact. **The relevant requirement is therefore not the exact reproduction of the generative development process but the reconstructability of the accepted relation among maintained project knowledge, the resulting digital research artefact, the referenced research-data state, and the documented grounds of acceptance.** Deterministic components such as preserved transformations, tests, or builds may remain reproducible, while model behaviour and human judgement cannot be replayed exactly. Reconstructability preserves the basis on which a promptotype can later be understood, criticised, continued, or revised, even though no documentation can capture every interaction, discarded alternative, or element of tacit competence.

## 4. Promptotyping in Practice

The documented projects do not constitute a controlled evaluation of Promptotyping. They differ substantially in material, purpose, technical setting, and maturity. They are treated as reconstructable cases that show how the method developed and where its operations became visible, rather than as comparable demonstrations of efficacy. A Promptotyping iteration may begin with an established corpus, the first records of an emerging dataset, a proposal-stage sample, or a failure discovered in an existing workflow. What recurs is the attempt to establish the identifiable relation defined in Section 2.2.

### 4.1 Cases and Evidential Status

The cases are reconstructed from combinations of repository documents, journals, decision records, code, tests, public interfaces, project pages, and author participation. These sources do not have equal evidential status. A public interface can establish that an artefact exists and show what it makes available, but it does not by itself establish the internal development history of a Promptotyping iteration. Claims about write-back, validation, and acceptance therefore remain tied to surviving project records. The selection is also biased towards projects that reached inspectable states and does not provide a systematic record of abandoned work or unsuccessful alternatives.

The cases were selected because each makes a distinct methodological problem visible. SZD-HTR foregrounds checking within production-scale transcription. CorrespExplorer shows how generative exploration still requires scholarly selection. M³GIM demonstrates feedback between an emerging data model and its operational representation. ZBZ documents a failure in the allocation of authority and its correction through write-back. Notker shows purpose-specific acceptance before a larger project had been funded. coOCR/HTR and teiCrafter show how bounded scholarly tasks can be organised in inspectable workbenches.

### 4.2 Artefact Forms and Documented Projects

Browser-based artefacts recur because they make project knowledge operational in a directly inspectable form. Their dominant scholarly functions can be distinguished without treating the categories as exclusive.

**SZD-HTR.** The pipeline, its heterogeneous source material, and its differentiated checking states are described as a worked example in Section 2.3.[^29] Exploration across the material showed that the central problem was not only transcription quality but the operational separation of generated output, model-mediated review, and authorised human examination. These findings were distilled into an annotation protocol and verification concept. The case shows that production-scale checking depends on maintaining the provenance and authority of different checks rather than reducing them to one confidence value.

**CorrespExplorer.** CorrespExplorer is a client-side environment for exploring correspondence metadata in CMIF through coordinated maps, timelines, correspondent views, lists, topics, and places.[^30] Generative implementation broadened the range of possible representations, but the resulting views still had to be selected against domain-specific criteria. The interface made modelling consequences concrete, particularly where uncertain dates expressed through `notBefore` and `notAfter` could not warrant precise points on a timeline. The retained design represents them as intervals. The project journal also recorded qualitative degradation during long implementation sessions, leading to shorter sessions, refreshed working context, and stronger reliance on maintained documents. Write-back therefore concerned both the representation of uncertainty and the organisation of agentic work.

**M³GIM.** M³GIM developed its RiC-O and JSON-LD data model, capture practice, and interface in parallel. Early exploration and capture environments gave project partners a shared operational representation of an emerging model before its vocabulary and corpus had stabilised. Their use exposed ambiguities and missing distinctions that had remained less visible in schema discussions. Findings were entered into the decision register and journal and were either written back into the data model or confined to the interface where they concerned presentation rather than domain semantics.

**teiCrafter.** The name teiCrafter refers to an early Custom GPT experiment and a later browser-based TEI workbench, which should be treated as distinct software states.[^34] The current workbench operationalises a bounded path from source material to inspectable TEI through generation or transformation, schema-aware checking, expert correction, and export. Its mapping rules function both as instructions for agentic work and as objects of scholarly validation. Schema validity can establish formal conformance but not textual, structural, or semantic adequacy, which remains subject to editorial judgement.

They do not establish independent long-term use, maintenance, or validation. Collaboration cases show that scholarly and technical responsibility can be distributed, but documented third-party continuation remains limited and must be evaluated through sustained project work rather than workshop completion alone.

## 5. Scope, Limits, Evaluation, and Conclusion

The documented cases support a bounded claim. They show that the method is workable within the practice from which it was consolidated, not that it outperforms alternatives or transfers beyond it. This chapter draws the consequences of that distinction. Section 5.1 delimits the empirical scope of the evidence and the conditions under which the method can responsibly be applied. Section 5.2 states the open questions of transferability and defines the criteria against which effectiveness would have to be evaluated. Section 5.3 concludes by returning to the problem with which the paper began.

### 5.1 Scope, Limits, and Conditions of Applicability

The method was consolidated primarily through projects led by one hybrid scholar-developer. There is no control condition, no common evaluation protocol across cases, and no systematic record of failed or abandoned attempts; as noted in Section 4.1, the surviving repositories are biased towards work that reached an inspectable state. Model capabilities also changed during the period in which the projects were developed. Observed improvements cannot therefore be separated cleanly into effects of the method, increased practitioner experience, deeper project attention, or stronger models.

**Promptotyping cannot externalise knowledge that a practitioner does not possess or cannot recognise as relevant.** A well-structured template may support articulation, but it cannot supply missing domain competence. Weak or misleading project documents can guide an agent towards an equally weak or misleading implementation.

The method likewise depends on structured and sufficiently contextualised research data. FAIR and semantic preparation can make relevant structures more explicit, but they do not remove the need to examine how the data were constructed, whose interests they represent, what they exclude, and how their provenance affects interpretation.

Beyond the limits visible in the documented artefacts, agentic organisation creates substantial coordination and auditing work. Monitoring several agents can become cognitively demanding, although this has not been measured systematically. Maintaining the knowledge base, auditing outputs, and documenting decisions add further effort. Reduced implementation effort may therefore be accompanied by less visible labour in context preparation, diagnosis, and validation. **Promptotyping redistributes labour rather than making it disappear, and the burden may fall unevenly across project roles.** Evaluation must account for who performs that labour and which competencies it requires.

The documented practice depends on paid access to proprietary frontier systems and suitable agentic tools. The paper does not test how far the method remains effective with local or less capable models. This dependence creates costs and reduces control over the systems on which present implementations rely, and it sits in tension with the inspectability and durability sought for research data and software. It is treated here as a methodological limitation of the documented practice. The concentration at issue extends beyond model provision. Proprietary infrastructures increasingly mediate many forms of research knowledge work, which can narrow available representations while placing important operations within systems whose defaults are only partly inspectable. The paper identifies this as a tendency requiring empirical and normative examination, not as an established monopoly.

These inequalities are one practical consequence of the asymmetric amplification discussed in Section 3.3. Researchers with well-modelled data, relevant expertise, time for validation, and access to capable systems may obtain greater benefits than those without these resources. Promptotyping inherits these inequalities and may intensify them when implementation capacity becomes concentrated among those able to access and govern high-capability systems.

Environmental cost and data governance also constrain responsible use. Promptotyping does not provide a general licence to submit research material to an external service. The permissibility of a workflow depends on the material, the legal and institutional basis for processing it, and the deployment architecture. Sensitive or restricted data may require local processing, synthetic substitutes, or the exclusion of particular systems. Promptotyping is inappropriate where lawful processing, accountable validation, or adequate communication of the artefact’s status cannot be established.

The practical position taken here is one of critical engagement. Current frontier systems enable the work described in this paper, while the need for open, accountable, and sovereign research infrastructure remains unmet. Competence in specifying, steering, and checking these systems is necessary for responsible present use, but it is not a substitute for reducing infrastructural dependence or for deciding that a given use is inappropriate.

### 5.2 Transferability and Priorities for Evaluation

The core mechanics of Promptotyping are not obviously limited to the humanities. Versioned project knowledge, agentic implementation, formal verification, and expert validation can in principle be applied wherever structured research data must be translated into a project-specific computational form.

The method was nevertheless developed against a specifically humanistic difficulty profile marked by heterogeneous sources, interpretative modelling, and contested representation. Fields with more regular schemas may face a less demanding version of the problem, but this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated transfer.

The project inventory contains applications in economic and social-work research, yet these cases do not establish general applicability beyond the digital humanities. Testing transfer requires practitioners from other fields and evaluation criteria appropriate to their research practices.

**The most important open question is whether a domain expert without a programming background can use the method to produce and validate an artefact of scholarly value without continuing hybrid assistance.** The teaching cases make this question visible but do not answer it.

Independently of generative implementation, the knowledge base can support handover to human developers and Research Software Engineers by preserving the project’s data description, requirements, decisions, and unresolved questions.

Future evaluation should distinguish three objects: the quality of the knowledge base, the quality of the resulting promptotype, and the performance of the method as a socio-technical process. Treating them as one outcome would obscure whether a failure originated in the project knowledge, implementation, checking, or organisational conditions.

* **Knowledge-base quality** concerns whether the maintained documents are coherent, current, traceable, and sufficient for another contributor or agent to continue without undocumented explanation. It should also be assessed by how clearly unresolved issues and decision rationales remain visible.  
* **Promptotype and artefact quality** concerns functional conformance, fidelity to the research material, representational adequacy, usability, and verifiability. Formal checks alone are insufficient because an artefact can pass tests while representing its material inadequately.  
* **Method quality** concerns how effort and intervention are distributed across the workflow. Evaluation should examine the human work required to detect and correct failures, the extent to which corrections become reusable project knowledge, dependence on particular models or harnesses, and the ability of another contributor to continue the project.  
* **Transferability** should be assessed through independent use rather than workshop completion. Domain researchers should be given structured data, the method specification, and document templates, then observed as they create and validate a promptotype without sustained intervention by the originating scholar-developer. Evaluation should record both the resulting artefact and the conditions under which outside assistance remains necessary.  
* **Comparative evaluation** should test Promptotyping against plausible alternatives such as direct conversational prompting, conventional development, notebook-based analysis, and other specification-driven agentic workflows. The aim is not to identify a universal winner but to determine when maintained project knowledge, write-back, and versioned acceptance add value.

A follow-up study should implement this comparison. Participants with different levels of technical expertise should undertake comparable bounded tasks. Evaluation should cover the quality of the resulting knowledge and artefact, the detection and correction of errors, the effort required for maintenance and checking, and the quality of handover. Model and harness should be recorded as experimental variables, and failed attempts must be retained to avoid reproducing the survivorship bias of the present inventory.

### 5.3 Conclusion

The paper opened with a CSV file that becomes a network in *Gephi* and with project-specific TEI that does not. Working with digital research data has long meant adapting them to the distinctions a generic tool can process, and accepting the loss of what it cannot. Promptotyping reverses this direction where the reversal can be warranted. Instead of translating research data into the categories of existing software, articulated project knowledge guides the development of artefacts that operationalise the project’s own distinctions, and each accepted promptotype makes the result inspectable in its relation to that knowledge, the referenced research-data state, and the documented grounds of acceptance.

The change this enables is modal rather than economic. Project-specific artefacts, comparisons of alternative operationalisations, and functional demonstrators become practicable in settings that previously could not resource them at all. Whether the method also improves outcomes where development capacity already exists remains an open empirical question, declared but not answered here.

More capable models will not make this organisation obsolete. As agents complete longer spans of work between human interventions, more of a project’s intent and criteria of assessment must be made explicit, and the distance between what a system can plausibly produce and what a scholar can responsibly accept grows rather than shrinks.

The method’s limits remain those set out above. Within them, the documented practice shows a workable form of AI-assisted research software development. **Promptotyping therefore changes the form in which scholarly knowledge guides implementation without transferring scholarly authority to an AI agent.** The contribution is amplification, not replacement.
