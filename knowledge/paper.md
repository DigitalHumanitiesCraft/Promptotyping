# Promptotyping. Translating Research Data into Research Artefacts through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering

## Abstract

Digital research data become operational for scholarly work through software, yet generic tools do not necessarily support the project-specific distinctions on which that work depends. Developing software adapted to particular research questions, data models, and scholarly practices has traditionally required resources that individual researchers and small projects often lack. **This paper presents *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering.** Its organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base from which LLM-based agents create and revise artefacts and into which findings and corrections from implementation, verification, and validation are written back. Responsibility for the consequential judgements through which each iteration is validated and accepted remains with accountable contributors. The paper consolidates a method developed through recorded experimental practice since 2023, led by a single scholar-developer across Digital Humanities projects and teaching contexts. While the documented cases demonstrate the method’s feasibility and range within this practice, its comparative effectiveness and transferability remain to be evaluated.

## 1. Introduction

Scholarly work with digital research data is necessarily mediated by software. Research activities such as enrichment and analysis may require the operationalisation of selected research concepts through procedures that make their instances identifiable, measurable, or available for downstream processing (Pichler and Reiter 2022). More broadly, enrichment, analysis, and dissemination[^1] rely on representations and procedures through which modelled distinctions become processable, queryable, and presentable by software. A CSV file can be opened as searchable text, interpreted as a sortable table in a spreadsheet, or imported into *Gephi*[^2] as a node or edge table when its records and attributes conform to structures the software can process. Relations encoded in project-specific TEI,[^3] by contrast, can be represented as a network only after relevant entities, relations, and uncertainties have been interpreted and mapped to a graph model and a compatible input format. A tool such as *Gephi* can process only those distinctions that have been expressed through graph structures and attributes supported by its representational and operational model. Project-specific semantics and uncertainty can therefore inform software-supported analysis and visualisation only insofar as they have been encoded in structures that the tool can process.

Large language models (LLMs)[^4] can reduce parts of the effort required both to map research data to the models and formats expected by existing software and to implement more closely adapted ways of working with those data. Structured or sufficiently semi-structured data can often be transformed deterministically through an explicit mapping. Where the relevant structure is not yet explicit, it must first be recognised or interpreted. If an LLM or another machine-learning system performs this step, the resulting output is probabilistic. Generative models can accordingly be used at different stages of research workflows in which digital research data are created, transformed, enriched, analysed, or made accessible (Pollin et al. 2025). They may generate structured output, produce code for deterministic transformations, or combine probabilistic and deterministic operations within a workflow designed for the material and task.[^5]

Mapping data into an existing tool may require less implementation effort than developing a project-specific artefact, but it also confines subsequent work to the distinctions, operations, and forms of interaction that the tool provides. LLM-based code generation can reduce parts of the implementation effort that have traditionally made project-specific development difficult for individual researchers and small projects. It can therefore make it practicable to develop and compare a wider range of project-specific digital research artefacts, allowing software to be adapted more closely to particular research data, questions, and scholarly practices rather than requiring those practices to conform exclusively to generic tools.

These possibilities do not eliminate the need for verification and validation. Probabilistic model outputs may be unsupported or internally inconsistent, while deterministic transformations may be incorrectly specified or implemented. Technical verification assesses whether an output or implementation conforms to formalised requirements, such as a schema, test, constraint, or specified mapping. Scholarly validation assesses whether the underlying representation is warranted by the source material and adequate for its intended research purpose.[^6] An implementation may therefore be technically correct while failing scholarly validation because the requirement or mapping it faithfully realises is itself inadequate.

This paper introduces *Promptotyping*, an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge. The name combines *prompt* and *prototyping* and retains the established function of a prototype as a provisional implementation through which requirements and design possibilities can be examined and refined. Such artefacts may take different forms, including interfaces, transformations, workflows, and research tools designed to support particular scholarly questions, data models, and practices. **The paper asks how scholars, research software engineers, and AI agents can collaborate through documented project knowledge to iteratively develop project-specific digital research artefacts that help project contributors clarify requirements and examine which forms of scholarly work the project’s research data can support.** Section 1 formulates the problem and establishes the conceptual and technical basis for the method. Section 2 presents the method itself, including its knowledge architecture, recurrent forms of work, and forms of verification, validation, and acceptance, and examines its operation through complementary project cases. It also defines the *promptotype*, the identifiable and versioned state that an accepted iteration yields. Section 3 compares the wider documented corpus to identify recurring project conditions, methodological functions, and artefact forms, and examines the epistemic consequences of artefact-mediated scholarly enquiry. Section 4 defines the method’s evidential scope, the conditions of its responsible application, and priorities for evaluation and transfer, and concludes by returning to the problem with which the paper began.

### 1.1 Translating Research Data into Digital Research Artefacts through Scholar-Centred Design

*Digital research data* broadly include digitally encoded materials collected, created, transformed, or used in the course of research. The term does not designate a uniform or self-evident class of objects. What counts as research data depends on the disciplinary and project context in which material is selected and interpreted (Geiger 2024). This paper focuses on structured research data: selective representations in which particular aspects of research material are made computationally explicit and thereby available for defined operations. This dependence on selection and interpretation is particularly consequential in the humanities, where historical and cultural sources often resist complete or unambiguous representation. Features of these sources become addressable only through scholarly modelling decisions, including decisions about how entities are related and how missing or uncertain information is represented (Drucker 2011; Schöch 2013). Although research data may themselves be understood as digital research artefacts in a broad sense, this paper distinguishes the two by their function within the research process. ***Digital research artefacts*** **are project-specific operational forms through which researchers work with structured research data for a defined scholarly purpose.**

Modelling provides a basis for this operationalisation by making selected aspects of a research domain explicit and relating them to a particular scholarly purpose (Ciula et al. 2023). It may involve conceptual accounts of the domain and research context as well as formal data models that specify which distinctions can be represented and processed computationally (Flanders and Jannidis 2019). These distinctions may be expressed through project-specific models, community standards such as TEI, or Semantic Web technologies.[^7] Such formalisation can contribute to FAIR preparation (Wilkinson et al. 2016) by supporting the machine-actionability, interoperability, and reuse of research data across systems.[^8] It does not, however, determine how researchers should work with those data in a particular project. The same data may support different research questions and therefore require different operational forms.

Digital tool criticism has accordingly shown that software participates in scholarly knowledge production rather than serving as a neutral means of access (van Es, Wieringa, and Schäfer 2018; Koolen, van Gorp, and van Ossenbruggen 2019; Fickers 2020; Herrmann et al. 2023). Translating research data into a digital research artefact is therefore not merely a conversion between technical formats. Software shapes which modelled distinctions become visible and what researchers can do with them. Generic tools provide predefined representations and forms of interaction that may not correspond to a particular project’s data and scholarly practices. Researchers may consequently have to adapt their inquiry to assumptions developed for other purposes. Project-specific digital research artefacts remain selective, but allow those selections to be made and examined in relation to the project’s own data and purpose.

Scholar-Centred Design was developed in my dissertation as a method for translating scholarly perspectives into project-specific requirements for digital research artefacts (Pollin 2025a, 89–98). It combines requirements engineering with sustained engagement with scholars, their source material, and the modelling decisions through which the data were produced. The method developed through work on the *Bookkeeping Ontology*[^9] and DEPCHA,[^10] which used a shared model of historical accounting transactions to normalise and publish heterogeneous datasets (Pollin 2019). Two contrasting use cases showed why this interoperable basis was not sufficient. Work on the *Wheaton Day Book* required ways of tracing social relationships and forms of exchange through economic transactions, whereas work on the *Jahrrechnungen der Stadt Basel* required aggregation and comparison across administrative and temporal hierarchies. The shared representation of transactions supported both forms of inquiry but did not determine which distinctions should be foregrounded or which operations and interface structures were required for each investigation.

These requirements could be specified, but the project lacked the development capacity to realise them as functional, project-specific artefacts. Translating scholarly requirements into software has commonly involved collaboration between domain specialists and technical contributors, including Research Software Engineers (Edmond 2005; Baxter et al. 2012; Cohen et al. 2021; Kemman 2021). Such collaboration remains essential for artefacts that require dependable long-term operation, institutional integration, security, or supported use by third parties. The necessary expertise and resources, however, are not equally available to individual researchers and small projects (Carver et al. 2022).

LLM-based systems can reduce parts of the implementation work, making it more practicable to develop project-specific digital research artefacts within the research process. Not all relevant requirements can be determined in advance. Provisional artefacts make proposed distinctions and possible ways of working with the data available for examination. They can reveal problems in the specification, show what the data can and cannot support, and help researchers compare alternative approaches. **Implementation thereby becomes part of determining both how researchers wish to work with their data and what those data can support.** Promptotyping organises this iterative process through maintained project knowledge that guides human contributors and LLM-based AI agents as they develop and revise artefacts together, and into which findings from implementation are incorporated as revised project knowledge.

### 1.2 Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI Harnesses

LLMs have become increasingly capable of generating code and carrying out extended software-development tasks.[^11] Their effective and responsible use, where such use is warranted, requires more than selecting a capable model or formulating an effective prompt.[^12] Results also depend on the information available to the system, the organisation of the work performed by LLM-based AI agents, and the technical environment through which they interact with a project. These interdependent informational, organisational, and technical concerns are addressed here through Context Engineering, Agentic Engineering, and AI harnesses.

*Context Engineering* refers here to the systematic selection, organisation, maintenance, and provision of the information an LLM-based system requires for its work. It extends *Prompt Engineering*, understood here as the iterative development of prompts through changes to their content or the techniques applied to them (Schulhoff et al. 2024), from individual prompts to the wider informational environment in which they are interpreted (Mei et al. 2025). Within Promptotyping, this environment is organised around maintained project knowledge that records the project’s current understanding of its data and purpose, together with decisions and unresolved questions relevant to implementation. Context Engineering does not consist in placing all available material into a context window. It involves maintaining an inspectable body of project knowledge from which task-specific context can be selected and refreshed.

An *AI agent* is understood here as an LLM-based system that pursues a goal through a sequence of tool-supported actions and adapts to intermediate results (Sapkota, Roumeliotis, and Karkee 2026; Wang et al. 2024). Rather than responding only to an isolated input, an agent may inspect project resources, act on them, evaluate the results, and continue its work across several steps. *Agentic Engineering* refers here to the systematic organisation of this extended work. It concerns how tasks are decomposed and coordinated, how agents use tools and respond to intermediate results, when human intervention is required, and how their work is inspected and continued. The term is used broadly because Promptotyping agents work not only on code but also on the documents through which a project is described, directed, and assessed.

An *AI harness* is the technical environment through which an agent receives context, accesses project resources, uses tools, and obtains feedback. It mediates the relation between the model and the environment in which the work is performed. Agentic capability therefore arises from the combined model–harness–environment system rather than from the model alone (Zhong and Zhu 2026). A harness may provide controlled access to project files, execution environments, tests, and running artefacts while maintaining state and permissions. It thereby supplies the technical conditions under which Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering can be applied.

Promptotyping is not tied to a particular AI harness or level of delegated agentic work. Its earliest cases were conducted through iterative sequences of prompts, with human direction renewed at each step. Contemporary agentic development systems combine a high-capability LLM with an AI harness that gives the agent controlled access to a persistent project environment.[^13] When this environment is maintained as a Git repository, project documents, code, configuration, tests, and references to relevant research data can be kept together under version control. The unit of agentic work is therefore no longer an isolated prompt or code fragment, but the versioned project environment as a whole. Beyond increased model capability, the methodologically relevant change is the growing amount and variety of work that can be delegated within a persistent, tool-accessible project context between successive human interventions.

As AI agents become capable of completing increasingly extended, multi-step computational tasks between human interventions, the knowledge required to guide their work can no longer remain confined to an individual prompt, a transient conversation, or continuous human direction.[^14] It must persist beyond individual interactions while remaining available to project contributors for inspection and correction.[^15]

## 2. Promptotyping as a Method

Promptotyping developed through LLM-supported experiments conducted in research and teaching since 2023. Early work externalised and distilled project knowledge into task-specific representations that could guide implementation across successive interactions rather than within individual prompts.[^16] As coding agents became able to operate within persistent project environments, such as Git[^17] repositories, these representations were consolidated into an evolving and versioned project knowledge base from which agentic implementation and revision could proceed. The term *Promptotyping* was first used in my dissertation and subsequently elaborated in a blog post and an article published on a scholarly portal (Pollin 2025a; Pollin 2025b; Pollin 2026a). The present paper consolidates the method, while its specification, document templates, and revision history are maintained in the public [Promptotyping repository](https://github.com/DigitalHumanitiesCraft/Promptotyping).

### 2.1 Promptotyping and the Project Knowledge Base

*Promptotyping* is an iterative, knowledge-driven method for developing project-specific digital research artefacts from structured research data and maintained project knowledge through Context Engineering and Agentic Engineering. Its organising structure is an evolving and versioned project knowledge base. Project contributors curate this knowledge and define and oversee the bounded implementation and revision tasks carried out by LLM-based agents. The artefact is developed through the continuing interaction of maintained project knowledge, agentic execution, and human interpretation and judgement. Findings arising from implementation and examination are written back into the knowledge base, allowing subsequent work to proceed from this revised understanding.

The method is knowledge-driven because implementation proceeds not from the research data alone but from a maintained account of how those data are understood and are to be operationalised for the project’s scholarly purpose. The data remain the project’s source basis and are processed through explicit, inspectable operations. The project knowledge base records the project’s current understanding of those data, its scholarly purpose, and the decisions governing implementation. It is a knowledge base in the sense of an explicitly represented body of knowledge that guides an agent’s work (Russell and Norvig 2020), although it takes a semi-formal documentary form and supports no formal inference. Rather than replacing the research data or reproducing them in full, it provides a task-relevant account of their meaning and of the conditions governing their interpretation and operational use.

The project knowledge base consists of interrelated documents. These are bounded representations distilled from fuller research material, maintained for human inspection and revision, and made available for inclusion in agents’ task-specific working contexts. The following three document types are organisational heuristics rather than a prescribed file structure, and projects may name, combine, and distribute their functions as appropriate.

* ***Declarative knowledge documents*** state what the project currently takes to be the case and what the artefact is required to realise. A `data.md` may describe the structure, semantics, provenance, gaps, and uncertainties of the research data. A `requirements.md` connects scholarly practices with expected system behaviour, while a `design.md` records how the project’s modelled distinctions should be represented and made actionable in the artefact.

* ***Process knowledge documents*** preserve how the project’s understanding developed and why decisions were taken. A `journal.md` may record attempted approaches, failures, and unresolved questions, while consequential decisions may be retained in dedicated records. Together with version control and informative commit messages, these documents make changes and their rationale traceable across the project.

* ***Agent instruction documents*** translate maintained project knowledge into operational guidance for agentic work. Files such as `CLAUDE.md`[^18] or `AGENTS.md`[^19] may define the technical baseline, working procedures, constraints, and checks governing an agent’s work. Keeping these comparatively volatile instructions separate from more stable accounts of the data and scholarly purpose prevents temporary implementation directives from silently altering the project’s maintained understanding.

Projects whose agents act across several persistent resources or handle consequential claims, rights, and release decisions may also maintain a versioned *project governance document*. This document records authority, permissions, evidential status, rights and release conditions, write-back targets, and escalation paths. Project governance is an optional documentary function whose need depends on the workflow. Its purpose is to make the conditions of agentic action inspectable without embedding them in temporary task instructions.

The project knowledge base may also be supplemented by *derived project artefacts*. These are regenerable outputs produced from a referenced project state through an identified process and stored in a format appropriate to their purpose. Their provenance and status may be recorded in document headers or accompanying metadata.[^20] They remain distinct from maintained project knowledge because they record derived observations rather than interpretations or decisions that project contributors have examined and adopted.

The persistent project knowledge base must be distinguished from an agent’s task-specific working context. The knowledge base preserves the project’s maintained understanding and decisions, while the working context contains the information and access required for a particular assignment. Relevant material may be drawn from maintained documents and derived project artefacts or obtained through retrieval and direct access to project resources. Retrieval-Augmented Generation may retrieve task-relevant material from larger collections and supply it as part of the working context (Lewis et al. 2020). This is particularly useful where those collections cannot adequately be represented through maintained summaries alone. These resources and access mechanisms may be combined, but they supplement rather than replace the maintained account of the project’s purpose, data semantics, and criteria of assessment.

Context Engineering organises the task-specific information and project access available to an agent, while Agentic Engineering structures how the agent acts within those conditions. The two are interdependent. A carefully maintained knowledge base does not determine an adequate course of action, and a well-organised agentic workflow cannot compensate for an inadequate account of the data or research purpose. Promptotyping is not tied to a particular documentary environment or mechanism for assembling working context. Project knowledge may be maintained in versioned files, an interlinked note system, or another inspectable and revisable form.[^21] Whatever arrangement is used, Promptotyping treats selection as part of Context Engineering rather than using accumulation as a substitute for it. The grounds for this restraint are examined in Section 2.2.3. The description does not replace the data, and access to the data does not replace the knowledge required to interpret them. An agent may therefore receive a distilled account and task-relevant selections from the research data, while inspectable computational operations process the complete dataset outside the model context.

The project knowledge base is therefore not prepared once and subsequently executed. It is established, examined, and revised through four recurrent forms of work that organise the development of a *promptotype*.

### 2.2 Forms of Work and Iteration

Promptotyping is organised around four recurrent phases of work: Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, and Implementation. These phases are analytically distinct but do not form a fixed or exclusively linear sequence. Their depth, order, and recurrence depend on the state and purpose of the project. Findings arising from Implementation may return the work to any earlier phase, most frequently to Distillation, where maintained project knowledge is revised.

#### 2.2.1 Preparation

Preparation assembles the source basis and research context from which subsequent work proceeds. It brings the relevant research material into an accessible project environment and makes its current status explicit. Analogue material may first require digitisation, while existing digital material may remain distributed across heterogeneous formats and levels of structure. Preparation does not require this material to be normalised, formally modelled, or placed directly in an agent’s working context. It requires the material to remain accessible for inspection and computational use, with its provenance and known limitations documented.

Preparation also gathers the scholar’s current understanding of the material and the project’s provisional purpose as documented in existing working materials. These may already express research questions or preliminary requirements, but their systematic formulation is not the primary purpose of Preparation. Exploration refines the relation between the available material and the intended scholarly work, while Distillation incorporates the resulting understanding into maintained project knowledge. Preparation is provisionally sufficient when the source basis and research context are digitally accessible and documented well enough to support systematic examination.

#### 2.2.2 Exploration

Exploration examines the prepared research material to understand its structure and limitations and to determine what the project knowledge base must represent. Once the source basis and research context are accessible, the material is examined for relevant patterns, variations, and unresolved problems. The purpose is not yet to formulate the maintained knowledge documents, but to establish what they need to contain for the project’s intended work.

The form of *Exploration* depends on the scale and structure of the material. A single text or a small collection may be examined directly, including through an LLM where appropriate. Large or heterogeneous collections may instead require computational profiling. For example, an LLM may generate code that extracts and aggregates structural information from thousands of TEI XML documents. The collection remains within the project environment, while the resulting profiles and selected examples can enter the agent’s working context.

The outputs of *Exploration* are provisional observations rather than maintained project knowledge. They become part of the knowledge base only through interpretation and Distillation. Exploration is provisionally sufficient when the material has been understood and documented well enough to determine what must be carried forward.

#### 2.2.3 Distillation

*Distillation* translates the understanding produced through Preparation and Exploration into maintained knowledge documents from which implementation and verification can proceed. It is the principal documentary operation of Context Engineering within Promptotyping. Distillation represents the project knowledge required for the current purpose while leaving the fuller sources and research data available for direct inspection and computational processing.

*Distillation* is not reducible to summarisation or context compression. A model’s nominal context capacity does not imply that all supplied information will be used reliably, and additional or poorly selected material may make relevant information more difficult to use (Liu et al. 2024; Hong, Troynikov, and Huber 2025). Distillation therefore does not seek merely to reduce token use. It creates an inspectable and selective representation that preserves the distinctions, conditions, and uncertainties required for adequate implementation and verification.[^22] It can consequently be understood as pragmatic modelling for human contributors and agent instances. The same material may need to be distilled differently when the project’s purpose or intended artefact changes.

The sufficiency of *Distillation* is practical rather than formal. A new contributor or agent instance, given the relevant knowledge documents and access to the project resources, should be able to reconstruct the project’s current logic and continue the assigned work without undocumented explanation. Difficulties in doing so can reveal omissions in the maintained account. Successful implementation, however, does not establish scholarly adequacy. A specification may be executable while resting on assumptions that cannot be justified.

#### 2.2.4 Implementation

Implementation makes maintained project knowledge actionable through LLM-supported development. This may proceed through iterative chat-based interactions in which contributors transfer prompts, code, and results between the model and the project environment. It is more effectively organised through an AI harness when the agent must work directly with project files, execute code, inspect intermediate results, and continue across several steps. In either arrangement, Implementation consists of bounded tasks through which proposed behaviour is realised and its consequences become available for examination.

Work proceeds through inspectable and versioned increments. Each increment should establish a runnable state that can be compared with the maintained project knowledge before further assumptions become embedded in the implementation. Early increments may prioritise making a transformation, interface, or workflow operational enough to support examination and discussion with project contributors.

Agentic implementation requires the project state to remain intelligible and testable as it develops. Generated code may require refactoring, while executable checks should be added or revised where relevant behaviour can be formalised. These practices are not merely subsequent technical clean-up; they preserve the inspectability of the implementation and make it easier to relate observed behaviour to the knowledge documents that guide it. Findings that alter the project’s maintained understanding initiate write-back to the relevant documents rather than remaining local corrections in the code.

Implementation is provisionally sufficient when the current artefact state is runnable, inspectable, and identifiable through versioning, so that it can support verification and discussion with responsible contributors. This state is not yet a promptotype; it provides the basis from which purpose-specific verification and acceptance can proceed.

A correction becomes methodologically consequential when it is incorporated into maintained project knowledge rather than confined to the current implementation. Write-back allows subsequent work to proceed from the revised understanding and is therefore not an additional phase, but the mechanism through which findings from Implementation become durable project knowledge. A Promptotyping iteration may contain several such cycles and concludes when the maintained project knowledge, resulting digital research artefact, referenced research-data state, and documented grounds of acceptance form a coherent and identifiable state for a stated purpose. **This accepted iteration state is a *promptotype*.** A runnable or plausible artefact alone does not meet this threshold if its relation to the project knowledge, data, and grounds of acceptance cannot be reconstructed. Acceptance remains the responsibility of the Critical Expert, understood as the person or group competent and accountable for judging whether the project knowledge adequately represents the research material and whether the artefact is suitable for its intended purpose. An agent may contribute proposals and assessments, but it cannot assume responsibility for their adequacy.

### 2.3 Applying Promptotyping: Complementary Project Cases

The preceding sections defined Promptotyping through its maintained project knowledge and the recurrent forms of Preparation, Exploration, Distillation, and Implementation. This section examines how these elements interact under different research conditions and at different stages of project development. The cases are presented as methodologically guided reconstructions of documented project practice rather than as complete histories or comparative evaluations. Each is analysed through the same elements: its initial project state, the interaction of the four forms of work, the findings arising from implementation, and their incorporation into revised project knowledge or purpose-specific acceptance. These elements provide a common analytical structure, not a fixed sequence. The analysis aims to show how Promptotyping operates under different research conditions and which methodological function implementation assumes in each case.

The first case brings together experimental workflows developed in the context of *Stefan Zweig Digital* (SZD)[^23] and the Zentralbibliothek Zürich project *Jeanne Hersch: Digitale Neuauflage der Schriften*.[^24] Both transform heterogeneous, multilingual source material into structured textual research data while keeping generated outputs and intermediate processing states available for inspection and correction. M³GIM[^25] provides a contrasting context in which early interfaces contributed to the development of a data model and capture practice for mobility information from the estate of the mezzo-soprano Ira Malaniuk. The Notker case[^26] examines a proposal-stage promptotype developed from a limited textual sample, editorial guidance, and an initial project description. Together, the cases show how implementation can support data production and curation, prospective modelling, and project formation under different research conditions.

#### 2.3.1 Agentic Data Production and Curation in the SZD and Jeanne Hersch Workflows

The experimental workflows examined here show how Promptotyping can organise agent-supported data production while keeping the relation among generated outputs, project knowledge, checking procedures, and responsible judgement inspectable. SZD works primarily with handwritten estate material, whereas the Jeanne Hersch workflow processes digitised printed writings.

*Preparation* brought the available sources and initial project requirements together in version-controlled working environments. In SZD, selected information from existing TEI records was inserted into the prompt supplied with each manuscript image. This included contextual details such as date, language, document type, writing instrument, and identified hand, while the classification of the object determined which task-specific transcription prompt Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite received.[^29] In ZBZ, the supplied source files, reference TEI, guidelines, and recorded project decisions were examined to distinguish relevant document and processing types and to formulate prompts for successive OCR, layout-recognition, and TEI-processing tasks. Preparation thereby translated existing scholarly knowledge into operational distinctions that could guide the treatment of particular sources.

*Exploration* examined how these arrangements worked in practice and where their assumptions failed. Researchers inspected selected facsimiles and generated outputs, while LLM-based agents investigated bounded samples by comparing images and transcriptions or tracing suspicious results back to their sources. Deterministic scripts extended this examination across larger sets of outputs and revealed recurrent structural or processing problems. In SZD, a controlled comparison showed that richer metadata could introduce information from the prompt into a transcription even when it was not visible in the image. In ZBZ, model-generated document classifications were treated as provisional hypotheses rather than authoritative descriptions. Agent findings likewise remained provisional until they had been connected to identifiable examples and interpreted in relation to the projects’ editorial aims.

The resulting understanding was distilled into the repositories’ maintained `knowledge/` directories. These documents record characteristics of the source material, processing requirements, annotation and checking conventions, methodological decisions, unresolved questions, and reasons for revising earlier approaches. In SZD, parts of this maintained knowledge are also processed by the build system and made accessible through the application. In ZBZ, the documentation records both the evidential value and the limitations of the supplied reference TEI, including phenomena that the available references do not represent and therefore cannot support as a basis for comparison. Distillation thus preserved uncertainty and negative findings rather than converting incomplete evidence into apparently settled requirements.

*Implementation* proceeded through successive versioned changes to prompts, processing routes, data representations, checks, and interfaces. Both repositories retain evidence of alternatives that were tested, restricted, replaced, or abandoned. Their current public deployments should therefore be understood as the latest inspectable states of documented development histories rather than as the direct execution of stable initial specifications. Findings from implementation were written back into maintained project knowledge, while revised knowledge informed subsequent implementation.

The examination of these implemented workflows required several forms of checking with different evidential scope and authority. In scholarly research, adequacy depends not only on conformity to specified requirements but also on the interpretation of sources, the modelling decisions through which they are represented, and the purpose for which the resulting data are intended.

* ***Deterministic verification*** tests conformity to formalised requirements through schemas, constraints, transformation tests, structural audits, and reproducible measurements. Its conclusions are limited to the properties encoded by the check and do not establish that the requirements themselves are adequate.

* ***Agentic review*** is a bounded, tool-supported investigation in which one or more LLM-based agents examine outputs, data states, implementations, or research artefacts against relevant sources, references, requirements, and criteria. It may involve locating project files, comparing sources and outputs, executing formal checks, investigating discrepancies, or coordinating specialised, parallel, or adversarial reviewers.

* ***Critical Expert verification and adjudication*** involves accountable examination of particular outputs against their sources and the resolution of cases that deterministic or probabilistic procedures cannot determine. It can confirm, correct, or reject previous findings and records who assumes responsibility for the resulting judgement.

* ***Scholarly validation*** assesses whether the representations, requirements, evaluation criteria, and artefacts governing the workflow are warranted by the research material and adequate for their intended scholarly purpose.

The term *agentic review* is used here in extension of emerging agent-based approaches to code review and other forms of tool-supported technical inspection.[^28] It also retains the broader sense of review as the criteria-guided and substantive examination of an artefact, as found in scholarly reviews of digital editions and resources.[^30] Whereas an LLM-as-a-Judge typically evaluates a supplied output under a given reference or rubric and returns a score, ranking, or judgement, an agentic reviewer can investigate a delimited part of a project by retrieving relevant materials, comparing several artefact layers, executing tools, and examining the grounds of apparent discrepancies. LLM-as-a-Judge can therefore be one operation within agentic review, but does not exhaust it.

The evidential value of agentic review depends on how the investigation is organised. Agentic Engineering determines how review tasks are delimited and decomposed, which project knowledge and sources are supplied, which tools and permissions are available, how several reviewers are coordinated, and when unresolved cases are escalated for human adjudication. Agentic review can consequently extend the scope and depth of inspection, but its findings remain probabilistic evidence rather than authorised verification, scholarly validation, or acceptance.

The differences among the four forms became consequential in both projects. In SZD, deterministic tests protected data contracts and the distinction between agent and human review states. Agentic review helped identify suspicious output patterns and directed attention to particular image–text discrepancies, while human examination corrected readings whose significance could not be determined through formal or statistical checks alone. Scholarly validation governed broader decisions about uncertainty, additions, transcription conventions, and TEI representation. In ZBZ, an earlier agent-screening process assigned approval labels even though no responsible contributor had granted approval. These labels were subsequently abolished, and agent findings were reclassified as provisional evidence pending operator adjudication. The capacity to inspect an output was thus separated from the authority to record it as verified.

The use of character error rate in ZBZ further illustrates the relation between deterministic verification and scholarly validation. Once the reference text, extraction rules, normalisation, comparison scope, and calculation procedure have been specified, CER can be calculated reproducibly. It nevertheless measures deviation from a selected reference rather than objective transcription correctness. The choice and quality of the reference, the treatment of corrections and annotations, the exclusion of particular textual phenomena, and the interpretation of the resulting value remain technical and editorial decisions. A deterministic metric can therefore verify a formally specified comparison without validating the scholarly adequacy of its specification.

Acceptance remains distinct from these forms of checking. It is the accountable decision through which an identifiable iteration state becomes a promptotype for a stated purpose (Section 2.2.4). Acceptance may accordingly be bounded: an artefact can be accepted as an experimental processing pipeline or handover state without being accepted as a completed or publication-ready scholarly edition.

The two workflows show that agent-supported data production becomes methodologically accountable not through automation alone, but through a reconstructable relation among sources, maintained project knowledge, versioned implementation, differentiated evidence, and responsible scholarly judgement. Their methodological significance lies not only in generated data and functional interfaces, but also in rejected assumptions, detected validation gaps, withdrawn approval states, corrected readings, and documented limitations that inform subsequent work.

#### 2.3.2 Prospective Modelling and Exploration in M³GIM

M³GIM shows how *Promptotyping* can operate while a project’s data model and requirements are still developing. The project investigates the mobility and professional life of the mezzo-soprano Ira Malaniuk through archival material from her estate. Rather than implementing a completed model, the project developed data capture and interface functionality alongside it. Implementation thereby became a means of examining and revising the emerging model in practice.

*Preparation* established a provisional basis for this work by bringing together available archival descriptions, initial records, modelling assumptions, and research interests. This basis was sufficient to begin examining the material and proposed distinctions, but neither the vocabulary nor the corpus was treated as complete.

*Exploration* proceeded through early functional representations of the emerging model. Rapidly developed interfaces allowed project partners to enter, display, filter, and compare information through a shared operational environment rather than discussing the model only through schemas and abstract descriptions. These interfaces made selected parts of the model actionable while leaving its categories, relations, and capture requirements open to revision.

*Implementation* made the consequences of provisional modelling and capture decisions available for examination. Categories could be assessed through the records assigned to them, the relations they permitted, the information they required during capture, and the representations they supported. Maps, timelines, networks, and related views showed how particular decisions organised the material and which interpretative claims those representations might appear to make.

Interaction with the interfaces revealed where distinctions could be supported by the available material, where categories remained ambiguous, and where additional information or qualification was required during capture. It also helped project partners examine which forms of inquiry the developing data could plausibly support and where a technically possible visualisation would imply a degree of precision, completeness, or certainty that the data could not warrant.

The resulting findings had to be interpreted before they could be written back. Some concerned the represented domain and required revision of the data model or capture practice. Others concerned how an otherwise adequate distinction should be made accessible and could remain within the operational or visual design of the interface.

Consequential findings were recorded in the project journal and decision register and distilled into revised project knowledge. Revised knowledge then informed subsequent modelling, capture, and implementation. The case therefore makes the prospective function of Promptotyping visible. A digital research artefact can participate in the formation of the model, capture practice, and requirements from which it is developed, provided that the consequences disclosed through its use are interpreted, documented, and incorporated into maintained project knowledge.

#### 2.3.3 Proposal-Stage Promptotyping in Notker

The *Notker* case shows how *Promptotyping* can support project formation before a complete corpus, production workflow, or funded development process exists. It began from a deliberately bounded source and knowledge base rather than from the materials required for a complete digital edition. Its purpose was to make a proposed editorial and technical arrangement functional enough to be examined and communicated within the preparation of a funding proposal.

*Preparation* brought together a limited textual sample, editorial guidelines, an emerging source model, and a Word document describing the proposed edition. These materials established the textual and organisational basis of the demonstrator while leaving corpus-wide modelling, transformation, and verification outside its stated scope.

*Exploration* examined how the proposed textual layers, annotations, translations, sigla, and modes of access might be represented in a functional artefact. The developing interface made it possible to compare alternative arrangements and identify requirements that remained implicit or ambiguous in the project description.

*Distillation* translated the emerging editorial and organisational account into requirements and design decisions that could guide transformation and interface development. Implementation then produced a bounded path from the initial material to a TEI representation and from that representation to a statically hosted Edition Interface.

The resulting artefact made the proposed relation among sources, textual layers, scholarly annotation, and modes of access available for inspection and discussion. It allowed the planned project to be encountered as a functional arrangement rather than only as a textual description. Implementation thus supported project formation by exposing the practical consequences of the proposal before a production corpus and workflow had been established.

The resulting promptotype was accepted as a demonstrator for a funding proposal, not as a completed or publication-ready edition. This purpose-specific acceptance did not establish corpus-wide philological adequacy and did not imply that the provisional transformation, model, or interface could be transferred unchanged into later production. The case shows that acceptance can stabilise a deliberately bounded project state for communication and decision-making without extending its claims beyond the materials examined and the purpose for which it was developed.

#### 2.3.4 From Complementary Cases to the Promptotype

The cases begin from different project states and foreground different functions of *Implementation*, but each maintains a reconstructable relation among maintained project knowledge, a referenced research-data state, the resulting digital research artefact, findings arising from its examination, and responsible judgement. SZD and ZBZ develop inspectable pipelines for producing and curating structured research data. M³GIM uses early interfaces to participate in data modelling, capture, and the formation of research requirements. Notker translates a proposal-stage concept into a bounded functional demonstrator. Promptotyping consequently prescribes neither a single project chronology nor one artefact form.

Maintained project knowledge guides *Implementation* without determining a single adequate realisation. Natural-language descriptions retain ambiguity, and different agent runs may realise the same requirement in materially different ways. The knowledge base therefore serves both as the reference from which implementation proceeds and as an account that can be revised when the resulting artefact exposes missing distinctions, unsupported assumptions, or inadequate requirements. Because formal conformity alone cannot establish the scholarly adequacy of such a realisation, Promptotyping requires the Critical Expert introduced in Section 2.2.4 to remain in the loop (Sterz et al. 2024). This responsibility extends beyond detecting errors in generated content to examining whether alternatives were excluded, conventions reproduced without justification, or absences concealed by an apparently coherent artefact.

Critical expertise may be held by one hybrid scholar-developer or distributed among contributors with complementary scholarly and technical competencies. Agentic work may likewise be divided among agents assigned to bounded components or checks, but this distribution does not transfer responsibility to them. Their assignments and permissions should remain explicit and auditable, and access to tools and project resources should be limited to the delegated task (Saltzer and Schroeder 1975; South et al. 2025). Their actions and outputs must additionally remain inspectable in relation to the project knowledge, sources, and criteria against which they are assessed. Increasing the number of agents does not reduce the need for responsible judgement and may increase the work required to coordinate and audit their actions.

An iteration is accepted when responsible contributors judge that it forms a coherent and identifiable state for a stated purpose, in the sense defined in Section 2.2.4. Acceptance is purpose-specific and does not imply that the project knowledge, research data, or artefact has become final. A promptotype may therefore be accepted as an exploratory interface, an internal production workflow, a proposal-stage demonstrator, a handover state, or a public research artefact. New sources, corrected assumptions, changed purposes, or improved checks may initiate another iteration and produce a later promptotype.

The accepted state must remain identifiable and reconstructable. Promptotyping does not require GitHub or Semantic Versioning, but the relation on which acceptance rests must remain recoverable through a repository release, archived deposit, or another durable reference. A renewed implementation using another model, harness, or project state constitutes a new iteration rather than a silent reproduction of the earlier promptotype. These complementary cases provide detailed reconstructions of Promptotyping but do not exhaust the documented practice from which the method was consolidated. The following chapter compares the wider corpus to identify recurring project conditions, methodological functions, artefact forms, and levels of findings.

## 3. Patterns across Promptotypes

The documented corpus extends the detailed cases with additional promptotypes developed for different materials, purposes, and stages of research. This chapter compares the corpus to identify recurring patterns without treating the projects as a controlled sample or each artefact as an independent evaluation of the method. It examines the conditions from which *Implementation* began, the methodological functions it performed, the operational forms of the resulting artefacts, and the levels at which their examination produced consequential findings.

### 3.1 Evidence and Comparison

The comparison draws on reconstructable cases documented through project knowledge bases, repositories, journals, decision records, code, tests, public artefacts, project pages, and the author’s participation in their development. These sources have different evidential scope. A public interface or runnable artefact can establish that a particular implementation state existed and show which operations it made available, but cannot by itself establish the history of that state, the decisions that informed it, the findings written back into project knowledge, or the authority through which verification or acceptance was assigned. Claims about development, revision, checking, and acceptance therefore remain tied to the surviving documentation. Some projects preserve extensive journals, decision records, and version histories, while others are represented primarily by an artefact, a bounded set of project documents, and references to the circumstances in which it was developed. The comparison consequently identifies documented patterns rather than reconstructing every project with equal precision.

Four analytical dimensions organise the comparison:

1. the **project condition** from which *Implementation* began
2. the **primary methodological function** performed by *Implementation*
3. the **operational form** of the resulting digital research artefact
4. the **level at which examination of the artefact produced a consequential finding**

These dimensions may overlap and change between iterations. A data-capture interface may also support exploration, while an edition environment may combine transformation, editorial examination, and verification. A production workflow may later become a handover package or itself become an object of methodological examination.

### 3.2 Project Conditions, Functions, and Artefact Forms

The documented promptotypes begin from substantially different project conditions. Some projects already possessed structured data, established models, and ongoing production workflows. Others began with an emerging model, a limited sample, provisional requirements, or a proposal-stage description of a future project. Promptotyping can therefore begin from an established research-data state, participate in the development of that state, or operationalise a prospective scholarly arrangement before a complete production corpus exists.

Across these conditions, *Implementation* performs three recurrent methodological functions. **Production and curation** make operations for creating, transforming, checking, correcting, and exporting research data functional and inspectable. SZD and ZBZ expose intermediate processing states, transformations, checks, corrections, and allocations of authority. coOCR/HTR and teiCrafter likewise combine transformation with bounded forms of verification and correction. Their methodological significance lies not only in producing outputs, but also in making the route from source material to differentiated output and checking states more explicit.

**Prospective modelling and exploration** use functional artefacts to examine an emerging model or the project-specific consequences of an existing representation. M³GIM makes developing categories and capture decisions available through data entry, filtering, comparison, and visualisation. CorrespExplorer makes relations, locations, and temporal uncertainty available for project-specific exploration. In both cases, interaction with the artefact allows modelling and representation decisions to be assessed through the data to which they apply.

**Project formation and bounded communication** give functional form to a proposed scholarly arrangement before production begins. The Notker demonstrator operationalised relations among sources, textual layers, editorial annotation, transformation, and interface design for the preparation of a funding proposal. Its accepted purpose was not to establish a completed edition, but to make the proposed project available for examination and communication.

These methodological functions are realised through recurring operational forms that may be combined within a single promptotype. **Capture** makes a data model actionable during structured data creation. **Transformation** maps source or research data into another explicit representation. **Exploration** supports investigation, comparison, filtering, and navigation. **Edition** makes an editorial account of texts and their relations operational through transformation, presentation, navigation, and annotation. **Verification** supports the inspection of outputs, evidence, checking procedures, and differentiated states. **Integrated scholarly workbenches** combine several of these forms for a bounded research task. M³GIM, for example, combines capture, exploration, and visualisation, while ZBZ, coOCR/HTR, and teiCrafter combine transformation with different forms of checking, correction, and editorial examination.[^34][^35]

**The recurring structure of Promptotyping therefore lies in the relation between maintained project knowledge and an artefact that makes selected scholarly operations available for examination.** No particular software form is constitutive of the method. Each promptotype gives functional consequences to a bounded account of the project’s data, purpose, requirements, and conditions of assessment. ### 3.3 Findings, Attribution, and Learning

The documented cases produced findings at several levels, not only within the implementation. These concerned the represented domain, source interpretation, research-data models, capture practices, transformations, interface representations, the organisation of agentic work, checking procedures, and the allocation of authority. These levels interact, but they are not interchangeable. Treating every observed problem as a defect in generated code would conceal the scholarly, technical, and organisational decisions through which the artefact was produced.

Findings concerning the represented domain, source interpretation, data model, or capture practice require revision of the project’s account of its material. In M³GIM, interaction with early interfaces showed where distinctions could be supported by the archival sources, where categories remained ambiguous, and where additional information or qualification was required during capture. A problem in the represented domain could therefore require revision of the model or capture practice, whereas a difficulty arising only from the display of an otherwise adequate distinction could remain a matter of interface design. This attribution prevents limitations of the model from being concealed through interface logic while avoiding unnecessary changes to the domain model. Although an implemented artefact makes the effects of several layers visible together, responsible interpretation must distinguish which layer produced the observed consequence.

Transformations and mappings require a corresponding distinction between conformity and adequacy. In teiCrafter, explicit mapping rules guide implementation while remaining available as objects of scholarly examination. A transformation may conform to its mapping and produce schema-valid TEI while still representing the source inadequately. Conversely, an error in the implementation of an adequate mapping should be corrected in the transformation rather than in the mapping itself. The implemented result thus allows conformity to the specified mapping to be examined separately from the scholarly adequacy of that mapping.

Findings in interface and visual representation concern the claims implied by the way data are made visible and actionable. CorrespExplorer showed that dates expressed through `notBefore` and `notAfter` could not warrant representation as precise points on a timeline.[^36] Representing them as intervals preserved uncertainty already encoded in the structured data. The resulting write-back therefore concerned the visual and operational treatment of uncertainty rather than the underlying correspondence records.

Other findings concerned the organisation of agentic work, checking, and authority rather than the research representation itself. The CorrespExplorer journal recorded qualitative degradation during extended implementation sessions, a finding taken up below as a method-level candidate. In SZD, ZBZ, and coOCR/HTR, distinctions among generated output, deterministic verification, agentic review, Critical Expert verification, scholarly validation, and acceptance made the allocation of authority operationally consequential. Problems at this level may require changes to working contexts, permissions, escalation procedures, agent instructions, verification states, or acceptance criteria rather than changes to the represented research domain.

**Write-back must therefore be directed to the level at which the underlying problem arises.** A finding may revise the account of the research material, a conceptual model, a capture practice, a requirement, a mapping, an interface decision, an agent instruction, a checking procedure, or the documented boundary of acceptance. Its purpose is not to preserve every observation, but to incorporate the interpreted consequence of a finding into the maintained project state from which later work proceeds. Not every consequential finding requires revision, however. Implementation may instead establish the boundary of what an iteration can responsibly claim. In Notker, the implemented arrangement was accepted for examining and communicating a funding proposal, while corpus-wide philological adequacy and production readiness remained outside its accepted purpose. Examination of the artefact thus supported a bounded acceptance decision rather than an unrestricted claim of completion.

Write-back differs not only in the level it addresses but also in its scope. Promptotyping distinguishes project-level write-back from revisions to the method itself. Project-level write-back changes the maintained knowledge, implementation conditions, or checking arrangements of a particular project, whereas method-level write-back incorporates a finding into more general rules for organising Promptotyping. A project-specific solution does not automatically constitute a transferable methodological requirement. Generalisation depends on the available evidence, the recurrence of the problem across cases, and the consequences of adopting the proposed rule; the method should not absorb every local workaround as a general prescription.

ZBZ provides the clearest documented instance of method-level revision. The withdrawal of agent-assigned approval labels described in Section 2.3.1 corrected the allocation of authority at the project level. It also supports a broader methodological rule: **agents may assemble evidence, compare materials, investigate discrepancies, execute checks, and record provisional assessments, but they may not independently assign an authorised verification status, scholarly validation, approval, or acceptance.**

CorrespExplorer provides a more provisional example. Qualitative degradation during extended implementation sessions prompted changes to session length, context refresh, and reliance on maintained project documents. At the project level, these changes concerned the organisation of agentic work; at the method level, the case suggests that implementation may reveal limitations not only in research representations and artefacts but also in the conditions under which context is selected and agentic work is continued. Further comparison is required before this observation can become a general rule. More generally, an observed failure becomes project- or method-level learning only through interpretation, attribution, documentation, and incorporation into the maintained project state. Without these operations, it remains a local implementation event rather than knowledge available to later iterations.

### 3.4 Implications and Evidential Limits

Across the documented corpus, *Implementation* repeatedly provided a functional setting in which assumptions about data, representation, workflow, and authority became available for examination. Digital Humanities scholarship has long treated prototypes, interfaces, and visualisations as forms through which interpretative claims and research possibilities can be explored rather than merely presented.[^37] The cases are consistent with this position because the resulting artefacts made selected distinctions and procedures actionable and their consequences available for examination through interaction. This pattern motivates the proposition that Promptotyping can support artefact-mediated scholarly enquiry.

Maintained project knowledge gives provisional form to a project’s understanding of its data and purpose, while *Implementation* translates part of that understanding into a functional artefact. Contributors can examine the consequences of the resulting arrangement, attribute observed problems to the source material, model, mapping, representation, workflow, or implementation, and incorporate consequential findings into revised project knowledge. The artefact does not produce scholarly knowledge autonomously. Its epistemic relevance arises through operationalisation, examination, responsible interpretation, attribution, and write-back rather than through generation or functionality alone.

Functional artefacts may provide a complementary way of examining modelling decisions by making some of their operational consequences accessible through records, views, and actions. Whereas a schema presents a model through formal notation, an interface can show how selected distinctions behave when data are entered, filtered, compared, and represented. M³GIM provides supporting episodes in which project partners examined categories through such operations rather than only through the model’s formal definition.

The documented practice does not establish that functional artefacts broaden participation, improve decisions, or produce findings that could not also have emerged through source criticism, specification review, data profiling, non-functional prototypes, or structured discussion. **Artefact-mediated scholarly enquiry therefore remains a conceptual proposition motivated by the cases rather than a demonstrated comparative advantage.**

The variation across project conditions, artefact forms, and bounded purposes supports the workability and range of Promptotyping within the practice from which it was consolidated. It does not establish causal effects attributable to the method, comparative superiority, independent transferability, or a complete typology of promptotypes. The evidential grounds of these limits and the conditions under which the open questions could be evaluated form the subject of the final chapter.

## 4. Scope, Conditions, and Evaluation

The documented practice supports a bounded claim about the workability of Promptotyping across the project conditions and artefact forms examined in this paper. It does not establish the method’s comparative effectiveness, general reliability, or independent transferability. This chapter defines that evidential scope, examines the competencies, labour, and infrastructures on which responsible application depends, and outlines how the project knowledge base, the accepted promptotype, and Promptotyping as a socio-technical process could be evaluated.

### 4.1 Evidential Scope and Applicability

Promptotyping was consolidated primarily through projects led by one hybrid scholar-developer and reconstructed from the surviving documentation of that practice. The cases do not constitute a controlled or representative sample. They were undertaken for different purposes under changing scholarly, organisational, and technical conditions, without a common evaluation protocol or systematic comparison condition. Their documentation is also subject to selection effects, since inspectable implementation states were preserved more systematically than abandoned, weakly documented, or unproductive experiments. Model capabilities, AI harnesses, practitioner experience, and the organisation of project knowledge also evolved alongside the projects. Observed improvements cannot therefore be attributed cleanly to Promptotyping rather than to more capable systems, better tools, increased experience, or more sustained attention. The corpus permits the reconstruction of recurrent methodological relations among maintained project knowledge, bounded *Implementation*, examination, write-back, differentiated checking, and purpose-specific acceptance, but not an unbiased estimate of the method’s causal effects or comparative performance.

The applicability of Promptotyping depends on the scholarly and technical competence available within a project, the condition of its research material, and the adequacy of its maintained knowledge. The method can support the articulation, implementation, and revision of available understanding, but it cannot supply domain knowledge that practitioners do not possess or cannot recognise as relevant. Templates and maintained documents may make assumptions more explicit and revisable, but they cannot compensate for missing competence. Research materials must also be sufficiently accessible and contextualised for the intended purpose. FAIR preparation and semantic modelling may improve technical usability, interoperability, and reuse, but they do not establish the adequacy of the underlying representations. Responsible application therefore still requires critical examination of the provenance, construction, scope, and representational limits of the data. Inadequate project knowledge can otherwise guide an agent towards an apparently coherent and operational implementation that remains inadequate in scholarly terms.

The maintained account of the project must be proportionate to the scope and consequences of the work being delegated. A bounded transformation operating on a stable and well-understood format may be guided by concise mappings and formal checks. Work spanning interpretation, modelling, implementation, and verification requires a more explicit account of the project’s sources, assumptions, requirements, and limitations. Promptotyping should not be treated as a substitute for collaboration with Research Software Engineers or other technical specialists. It may make project-specific implementation practicable earlier and clarify what later development must provide, but artefacts intended for dependable institutional or public operation continue to require appropriate expertise in security, accessibility, maintainability, integration, and sustained operation. Promptotyping is inappropriate where contributors cannot reconstruct or assess the relation among sources, research data, maintained project knowledge, implementation, and output. Lowering the barrier to implementation does not justify artefacts whose grounds, limitations, responsibilities, and status cannot be made accountable.

### 4.2 Labour, Infrastructure, and Responsible Use

Promptotyping redistributes implementation labour rather than eliminating it. An agent may produce an initial functional artefact rapidly, while domain specialists must still specify requirements, examine outputs, interpret discrepancies, and determine what can be accepted. Research Software Engineers may later need to refactor the implementation for dependable operation. Evaluation must therefore distinguish effort that is reduced from effort that is transferred, newly created, or deferred. Relevant work includes maintaining project knowledge, coordinating and examining agentic work, and preserving an intelligible and verifiable project state. The combined burden of these activities has not been measured systematically in the documented cases.

This redistribution may affect project roles unevenly. Domain specialists may assume additional supervisory work, Research Software Engineers may inherit technical debt, and those responsible for verification may confront more plausible outputs without corresponding resources or authority. Apparent productivity gains may consequently conceal transferred effort, deferred maintenance, or reliance on unusually hybrid expertise. This relation can be described as *asymmetric amplification*. Promptotyping may extend the practical reach of articulated scholarly and technical competence, but its benefits remain dependent on data quality, available knowledge, access to capable systems, and the capacity to examine their outputs.[^27] Researchers and institutions with stronger expertise, infrastructure, and verification capacity may therefore benefit disproportionately.

The documented practice also depends substantially on paid access to proprietary frontier models and capable agentic tools. The paper does not establish how far the method remains effective with local, open-weight, less capable, or more narrowly specialised systems. Proprietary dependence introduces direct costs, reduces control over system changes, and sits in tension with the inspectability, reproducibility, and durability sought for research software and data. Model and harness changes may alter system behaviour even where project knowledge and research data remain stable.

Data governance further constrains which materials and workflows can be used. Promptotyping does not provide a general licence to submit research material to external generative systems. Permissibility depends on the material, applicable legal and institutional conditions, and deployment architecture. Restricted, confidential, or personally identifiable material may require approved local processing, redaction, or exclusion from particular systems. Agent access to project resources and execution environments must likewise be bounded through controlled permissions and attributable actions. Consequential changes should remain inspectable and reversible, and purpose-specific scholarly acceptance does not replace institutional responsibility for secure, sustainable, and legally compliant operation.

The documented cases also do not permit claims about environmental efficiency, since the resource use of models, repeated generations, deployment architectures, and alternative workflows was not comparatively measured. Reduced development effort should not therefore be treated automatically as evidence of reduced resource consumption. Responsible use requires proportionate system selection and may include the decision not to apply Promptotyping to particular materials, systems, or workflows.

### 4.3 Transferability and Evaluation

The core relation among maintained project knowledge, bounded *Implementation*, examination, write-back, differentiated checking, and accountable judgement is not intrinsically limited to the humanities. It could in principle be applied wherever structured research data must be translated into a project-specific computational form. Promptotyping was nevertheless developed in response to a specifically humanistic difficulty profile involving heterogeneous historical and cultural sources, interpretative modelling, incomplete or uncertain evidence, and forms of adequacy that cannot be determined through technical conformity alone. Fields with more standardised data structures or settled criteria may encounter a less demanding version of this problem, while other domains may require statistical, regulatory, or safety procedures not represented in the documented practice. Transfer would therefore require adaptation rather than the unmodified adoption of the current method specification.

Applications involving economic, social-work, and other research contexts do not yet establish independent transfer beyond the Digital Humanities. A change of subject matter is insufficient where the same originating practitioner continues to organise and assess the work. The stronger test is whether new domain contributors can maintain and apply the project knowledge, diagnose failures, organise appropriate verification, and establish defensible grounds of acceptance without continuing undocumented support from the method’s originator. Teaching cases show that participants can work with structured documents and generated implementations under guided conditions, but they do not establish independent continuation across sustained project work.

Transferability must also be distinguished from exact reproduction. Another practitioner may apply the method while producing a different artefact with different documents, models, tools, and project structures. Successful transfer would not require identical outputs. It would require the core methodological relations to be established, applied, and reconstructed without undocumented intervention by the originating scholar-developer.

Future evaluation should distinguish three related objects: the project knowledge base, the accepted promptotype, and Promptotyping as a socio-technical process.

**Table 2. Objects of a future evaluation of Promptotyping.**

| Evaluation object | Central question | Indicative evidence |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Project knowledge base** | Can another contributor reconstruct the project’s current logic and limitations well enough to examine or continue the work? | Maintained documents, decision rationales, examples, known limitations, unresolved questions |
| **Accepted promptotype** | Does the accepted state form a coherent and reconstructable relation among project knowledge, research data, artefact, purpose, and grounds of acceptance? | Referenced data state, versioned implementation, checks, source comparison, expert assessment, acceptance record |
| **Socio-technical process** | How are work, intervention, learning, authority, and responsibility distributed? | Human work by role, write-back, external assistance, handover, model and harness dependence, maintenance requirements |

Knowledge-base quality concerns whether another contributor can reconstruct the project’s current logic, limitations, and unresolved questions well enough to examine or continue the work. Bounded continuation tasks can test this practical sufficiency. Independent contributors might be asked to explain the project, diagnose a selected discrepancy, or continue a delimited implementation task using the maintained documents and project resources. Observed difficulties should be attributed, where possible, to documentary, technical, access-related, or competency-related limitations.

Promptotype quality concerns whether maintained project knowledge, a referenced research-data state, the resulting artefact, and documented grounds of acceptance form a coherent and identifiable state for the stated purpose. Evaluation should distinguish technical conformity, scholarly adequacy, and suitability for that purpose. Tests and schemas can establish conformity to formalised requirements, while source comparison and responsible expert judgement are required where fidelity and interpretative adequacy are at stake. An artefact may pass its tests while embodying an inadequate mapping or misleading representation. Conversely, an exploratory or proposal-stage promptotype need not satisfy production-level engineering criteria where those criteria lie outside its accepted scope.

Process evaluation should examine how work and responsibility are distributed and whether findings become reusable project knowledge rather than remaining local repairs to a single implementation state. It should determine which forms of effort are reduced, transferred, newly created, or deferred, and how much external assistance is required to maintain, verify, and continue the project.

Coordination should form an explicit evaluation variable. Coordination theory defines coordination through the management of dependencies among activities (Malone and Crowston 1994), while organisation design research relates task uncertainty to information-processing requirements (Galbraith 1974). Controlled evaluations of language-model agent systems further indicate that coordination topology can affect overhead and error propagation (Kim et al. 2025). Evaluations of Promptotyping should therefore record dependency structure, coordination overhead, error propagation, handover volume, persistent write targets, and the distribution of checking responsibilities when they compare single- and multi-agent arrangements.

One candidate protocol separates research orchestration, which maintains scholarly questions, concepts, claims, sources, and acceptance criteria, from operational orchestration, which changes persistent artefacts and records their realised state. A versioned Research Brief can convey accepted scholarly commitments to implementation; an Implementation Report can return checks, deviations, and new findings for write-back. Future studies should test whether this arrangement improves traceability in workflows that revise scholarly commitments while changing persistent artefacts, and under which dependency structures its coordination cost exceeds its benefit. Its present status is a testable design hypothesis. The documented corpus does not establish it as a general method rule.

The proposed epistemic function of functional artefacts also requires comparative evaluation. Studies should compare findings emerging through *Implementation* with those produced through source examination, specification-based methods, and established development workflows. They should examine whether functional artefacts alter participation in modelling and design, what forms of expertise remain necessary, and whether the resulting decisions differ in quality, scope, or traceability.

Independent transfer should be evaluated through sustained project work rather than workshop completion or isolated prompting tasks. New domain contributors should receive the method specification, relevant project materials, and access to an appropriate technical environment, and should then develop and assess a bounded promptotype without continuing intervention by the originating scholar-developer. Evaluation should preserve successful, unsuccessful, and discontinued paths and record changes to project knowledge, artefacts, human intervention, external assistance, and grounds of acceptance. Model and harness should be treated as experimental variables rather than invisible background conditions, while later handover, continuation, revision, and maintenance should form part of the assessment.

Comparative evaluation should determine when maintained project knowledge, systematic write-back, differentiated checking, and versioned acceptance add value rather than seeking a universal winner. Relevant comparison conditions include less structured AI-assisted work, established research software development, and configurable research environments. An artefact that appears rapidly may still be difficult to examine, verify, maintain, revise, or transfer. Evaluation must therefore extend beyond the first functional implementation.

### 4.4 Conclusion

This paper opened with a CSV file that *Gephi* could process and project-specific TEI that it could not. That asymmetry is a structural condition of software-mediated research. A tool operationalises only those distinctions that have been translated into its representational model, and the direction of that translation has ordinarily run from the project towards the tool. Researchers must therefore map their data and questions into the representations and operations that existing software provides.

Promptotyping can partially reverse this direction under stated conditions. Maintained project knowledge records how the research data are understood and for what purpose they are to be operationalised. LLM-based agents working from that knowledge contribute to translating it into functional, project-specific artefacts, while findings arising from examination are interpreted and written back into the maintained project state. The *promptotype*—an accepted and identifiable iteration comprising maintained project knowledge, a referenced research-data state, the resulting artefact, and documented grounds of acceptance—is the form through which this process remains reconstructable and accountable. This paper applies that form to itself. Its load-bearing statements are anchored source by source in a machine-validated evidence layer published with the repository, and the Author's Note records the conditions of its production.

The significance of this capacity is modal rather than economic. The documented cases do not show that Promptotyping produces artefacts faster, more cheaply, or more reliably than alternative approaches, and the paper has not argued that it does. They show that forms of project-specific implementation which had previously lain outside the practical resources of individual researchers and small projects became workable at all. Requirements could be examined through functional artefacts, emerging data models could be tested against the material they were intended to organise, and proposed projects could be encountered as operational arrangements before production began.

Increasingly capable models and harnesses do not render the method obsolete. As the volume, scope, and plausibility of delegable work grow, the span between what agents can produce and what scholars can responsibly accept widens rather than narrows. Maintained project knowledge, differentiated checking, write-back, and purpose-specific acceptance are the means through which that span can remain governable. Their importance increases with the capabilities they organise and constrain.

These conclusions hold within the evidential scope defined in Section 4.1 and await the comparative and independent evaluation outlined in Section 4.3. Within that boundary, the paper’s position is that the contribution of LLM-based agents to scholarly work is amplification rather than a transfer of authority. Promptotyping amplifies the practical reach of articulated project knowledge; the scholarly competence and the accountable judgement it presupposes remain indispensable.
